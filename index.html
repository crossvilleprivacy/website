Quick FAQ

Do these cameras require a warrant?

No. Plates are scanned automatically. Searching travel history is usually a typed reason in the police system, not a warrant, unless the city writes stricter rules. More FAQ →

Is covering my plate legal in Tennessee?

No. T.C.A. § 55-4-110 archived Mar. 27, 2025 prohibits camera-blocking covers. Use council, email, and TPRA instead. Do not obscure your plate.

Flock Safety cameras & ALPR surveillance in Crossville, Tennessee

Public records and local reporting on Crossville’s Flock Safety automated license plate readers: how the city got the cameras (Chronicle archived Aug. 16, 2026), who oversees them, what they cost, and Tennessee misconduct cases that show what can happen when ALPR access lacks meaningful supervision.

How Crossville got Flock (2022)

In June 2022 archived Aug. 16, 2026, Crossville Police Chief Jessie Brooks told council and local media the department wanted Flock cameras as “extra eyes” for investigations, Amber/Silver alerts, and locating vehicles already in law-enforcement databases, not as red-light ticket cameras.

On December 13, 2022, City Council minutes record that Chief Brooks “suggests purchasing License Plate Readers” as an eligible expense on a state Violent Crime Intervention Fund grant application ($126,378). The same consent agenda records Police Chief Brooks recommending a Flock quote of $28,500 to purchase 10 cameras with installation. The minutes say a budget amendment was not necessary because the item was approved in the FY 2022–23 budget, and that it was not bid because the city treated Flock as a sole-source provider. Council approved consent items 5A–T except M on a recorded 4–0 vote (Shanks, Gernt, Crawford, Turner).

Sources: Crossville Chronicle (June 27, 2022) archived Aug. 16, 2026; 3B Media (Dec. 5, 2022) archived June 11, 2026; Crossville Council minutes (Dec. 13, 2022) · archived Aug. 18, 2026. Then-City Manager Greg Wood publicly framed the system as extra eyes for the department. City Manager today is Valerie Hale.

Effectiveness claims

Does this solve crime? What public records actually show

Vendors and some departments pitch ALPRs as a major crime-solving tool. Public-records releases and independent analyses tell a different story: these systems photograph nearly everyone who drives past, then match only a tiny fraction to wanted or stolen-vehicle lists, and even those “hits” are not the same as cleared cases. Crossville has not published local Flock results (arrests, recoveries, or cases closed because of these cameras). Ask for them under TPRA and our questions for City Council.

Hit ≠ solved crime. A “hit” usually means a scanned plate matched a hotlist entry (stolen, wanted, etc.) at the moment of capture, not that police made an arrest, recovered a car, or cleared a case. Search volume and solved-case rates are separate questions. Vendor “crimes solved” claims are marketing until the city publishes attributable local numbers.

Audit logs

Flock changed which fields appear in cross-agency audit logs

In November 2025, Flock advertised “immutable accountability”: every user action and search reason kept in an indefinitely available audit trail. On December 11, 2025, Flock’s Network Audit Logs stopped showing officer names, specific plates searched, vehicle fingerprints, and open-text search reasons. That change followed public scrutiny of audit data (including tools such as Have I Been Flocked that used those fields). Flock said the change was intended to protect officer safety and investigations. Reporting found it was also applied retroactively, with portal fields replaced by “REDACTED”, see sources below and misuse cases that relied on searchable logs.

Vendor marketing is not a Crossville audit. If the city leans on Flock’s transparency claims instead of publishing its own monthly search summary and a supervisor-approval rule, residents lose the fields that helped uncover personal-use abuse elsewhere. Ask for city-held logs under TPRA, and ask which fields Flock still delivers to Crossville PD.

  • Flock has said omitted fields remain available to the originating agency, which only helps Crossville if the city actually pulls, reviews, and publishes audit summaries.

How the Fourth Amendment is supposed to work, and how plate-reader networks sidestep it

Traditional path

  1. Specific suspicion of a specific person
  2. Articulate probable cause
  3. Get a warrant from a judge
  4. Then search

Flock ALPR path

  1. You pass one of Crossville’s Flock cameras (Quote Q-00234 lists 10 Falcons, not every street)
  2. Plate, image, time, and location are stored
  3. In typical Flock deployments, authorized users can type a “reason” and search (Crossville rules unpublished)
  4. You get no notice and no chance to object

TCA § 55-10-302 archived May 5, 2025 may limit retention windows, but it does not create a municipal immunity shield against 42 U.S.C. § 1983 archived Aug. 12, 2026 claims. Crossville still chooses whether to keep buying the product.

Key precedents & what dense Flock networks look like in court

  • United States v. Jones (2012) archived July 29, 2026: Justice Sotomayor’s concurrence warned that GPS-style monitoring can assemble a precise record of a person’s public movements and is “susceptible to abuse.”
  • Carpenter v. United States (2018) archived Aug. 12, 2026: historical cell-site location information generally requires a warrant; the Court recognized privacy in “the whole of [a person’s] physical movements.”
  • Schmidt v. Norfolk archived July 6, 2026 (Flock network; ongoing appellate fight). NBC News archived July 17, 2026 and the ACLU case page archived July 9, 2026 summarize dense-network tracking counts. Multi-Geo / Convoy tools: Flock dossier.

Do Flock / ALPR cameras actually solve much crime?

Public-records hit ratios are typically well under 1% of plate scans, and a “hit” on a wanted or stolen-vehicle list is not a cleared case. Public-records work by the Electronic Frontier Foundation and MuckRock, Spokane Flock audit math, and Atlanta FBI solved-case comparisons all undercut the sales pitch. Crossville has not published local results. Full writeup: Does this solve crime?.

Do Crossville Flock cameras require a warrant?

No. License-plate cameras scan vehicles automatically. Searching stored travel history is usually a lookup in the police system with a typed reason, not a warrant, unless the city writes stricter rules.

Has Flock ALPR been misused in Tennessee?

Institute for Justice reporting also says a Shelby County deputy was accused of using county ALPR databases (not confirmed here as Flock) more than 100 times to monitor a civilian. Is canceling Flock enough if the city hires Axon instead?

No. Multiple cities have dropped Flock and then installed Axon plate readers to keep scanning cars: Denver, Douglas County (CO), Grand Chute (WI), Syracuse (NY), and others. That changes the company name. It does not end mass plate scanning. Other vendors shop the same job (Motorola Vigilant, Rekor, Genetec, Leonardo ELSAG / SignalTrace). Axon products raise separate concerns too (AI report-draft tools that delete drafts; an Atlanta ethics case involving an official who owned a stake in Fusus while pitching it). Do not demo, trial, or “evaluate” Axon or any other plate-reader brand. Ask council to cancel Flock, remove the cameras, and refuse any plate-reader replacement. Fix this by taking the cameras down and requiring warrants for specific lookups, not by scanning every driver. Full writeup: Not just Flock · Other vendors · Gadget Review (July 20, 2026) archived Aug. 15, 2026.

Is Flock only license-plate cameras, or drones too?

Flock’s catalog already includes docked Drone as First Responder aircraft that launch to 911 calls, plate-reader hits, or gunshot alerts. On the Aug. 18, 2026 All-In podcast, CEO Garrett Langley told Business Insider archived Aug. 24, 2026 drones are now the company’s fastest-growing line, with more than 200 DFR customers, while ALPRs are under half of forward-looking revenue. Crossville has published Falcon plate readers. It has not published whether any Flock drone product is in the live contract. Ask council before assuming the local deal is “just cameras.” Full writeup: CEO / All-In.

Can Flock cameras be used to issue traffic tickets?

The 2022 Crossville pitch said they were not red-light ticket cameras. Flock’s own materials used to say the cameras are not used to enforce moving violations. That language was removed after December 2, 2025. In Georgia, a mailed phone-use ticket cited a Flock image. Crossville has not published whether traffic-ticket use is enabled here, or a written ban. Full writeup: Traffic tickets.

Does Flock track people, or only license plates?

Flock’s core product is vehicle LPR, but FreeForm adds natural-language search that can include people descriptions (for example clothing). The company markets that cameras are not face identification. Also, Multi-Geo and Convoy Search track cross-city travel and vehicles that move together, tools that track daily travel patterns, not “only a plate hit.” Crossville should disclose whether FreeForm, Multi-Geo, and Convoy are enabled.

What are Multi-Geo and Convoy Search?

Multi-Geo finds vehicles that appear in two or more cities inside a time window. Convoy Search flags vehicles that repeatedly show up near each other. East Tennessee training materials describe officers claiming probable cause (“got PC”) from a Detroit-Knoxville travel pattern alone. Full writeup: Multi-Geo & Convoy.

Were Flock livestreams really left open on the internet?

Yes, reporting in late 2025 documented dozens of Condor-class feeds (Flock people-tracking video cameras) discoverable via commercial search engines with no login. See the open livestream exposure section and its sources.

What are the “51 cybersecurity findings” about Flock?

Can Crossville trust Flock’s “immutable” audit logs?

Does Maine or Virginia have laws Tennessee could copy?

Yes. Maine broadly prohibits ALPR use except for narrow government and law-enforcement exceptions, caps ordinary data retention at 21 days, and makes violations a Class E crime. Virginia requires 21-day deletion unless tied to an active investigation, mandates written agency policies, audit trails, internal audits, public reporting, vendor restrictions, and limits on data sharing. Tennessee caps ordinary ALPR storage at 90 days under TCA § 55-10-302 but lacks that broader package. See the state-level ALPR comparison.

Does state retention law protect Crossville from lawsuits?

TCA § 55-10-302 archived May 5, 2025 is a storage cap. It is not a grant of immunity from federal § 1983 archived Aug. 12, 2026 claims if a stop is later alleged to be unconstitutional. That is a civic reading of the statute’s scope. It is not legal advice and not a prediction that Crossville will be sued or will lose.
