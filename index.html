Quick FAQ

Do these cameras require a warrant?

No. Plates are scanned automatically. Searching travel history is usually a typed reason in the police system, not a warrant, unless the city writes stricter rules. More FAQ →

Is covering my plate legal in Tennessee?

No. T.C.A. § 55-4-110 prohibits camera-blocking covers. See the Fight back guide for legal alternatives (council, email, TPRA, vehicle habits).

How Crossville got Flock (2022)

In June 2022, Crossville Police Chief Jessie Brooks told council and local media the department wanted Flock cameras as “extra eyes” for investigations, Amber/Silver alerts, and locating vehicles already in law-enforcement databases, not as red-light ticket cameras.

These systems often launch without meaningful public oversight

Across agencies, ALPR portals (Flock today; Axon Fleet / Evidence tomorrow) commonly let an officer open a search window, type a free-text “reason,” and pull travel history immediately. Supervisor approval is optional unless a city writes it into policy. Audit logs exist on paper; few Tennessee cities publish them, see audit-log redactions and Tennessee misuse. Switching companies does not create real oversight, see Not just Flock.

Typical ALPR plate search

  • Officer opens vendor portal (Flock, Axon Evidence, etc.)
  • Types any reason into a free-text field
  • Travel history returned instantly
  • No public monthly totals
  • Misuse usually found by internal complaint or journalist, not company “accountability” marketing

What Crossville residents can demand

  • Published written policy: who can search, why, retention, sharing
  • Supervisor approval before every plate search
  • Monthly public audit summaries
  • Hard ban on personal / romantic / conflict searches, see misuse cases
  • Full contract, renewals, camera map, and sharing list: TPRA

Effectiveness claims

Does this solve crime? What public records actually show

Vendors and some departments pitch ALPRs as a major crime-solving tool. Public-records releases and independent analyses tell a different story: these systems photograph nearly everyone who drives past, then match only a tiny fraction to wanted or stolen-vehicle lists, and even those “hits” are not the same as cleared cases. Crossville has not published local Flock results (arrests, recoveries, or cases closed because of these cameras). Ask for them under TPRA and our questions to ask before renewal.

Hit ≠ solved crime. A “hit” usually means a scanned plate matched a hotlist entry (stolen, wanted, etc.) at the moment of capture, not that police made an arrest, recovered a car, or cleared a case. Search volume and solved-case rates are separate questions. Treat vendor “crimes solved” marketing as marketing until the city publishes attributable local numbers.

Audit logs

Flock changed which fields appear in cross-agency audit logs

In November 2025, Flock advertised “immutable accountability”: every user action and search reason kept in an indefinitely available audit trail. On December 11, 2025, Flock’s Network Audit Logs stopped showing officer names, specific plates searched, vehicle fingerprints, and open-text search reasons. That change followed public scrutiny of audit data (including tools such as Have I Been Flocked that used those fields). Flock said the change was intended to protect officer safety and investigations. Reporting found it was also applied retroactively, with portal fields replaced by “REDACTED”, see sources below and misuse cases that relied on searchable logs.

Vendor marketing is not a Crossville audit. If the city leans on Flock’s transparency claims instead of publishing its own monthly search summary and a supervisor-approval rule, residents lose the fields that helped uncover personal-use abuse elsewhere. Ask for city-held logs under TPRA, and ask which fields Flock still delivers to Crossville PD.
  • Footnote4a published the Dec. 9 customer email from Flock VP Chris Colwell spelling out the field removals.
  • Unfiltered Ledger and Atlanta News First documented the oversight impact: cross-agency logs became far harder for the public to use to spot stalking.
  • Flock has said omitted fields remain available to the originating agency, which only helps Crossville if the city actually pulls, reviews, and publishes audit summaries.

How the Fourth Amendment is supposed to work, and how plate-reader networks sidestep it

Traditional path

  1. Specific suspicion of a specific person
  2. Articulate probable cause
  3. Get a warrant from a judge
  4. Then search

Flock ALPR path

  1. You pass one of Crossville’s Flock cameras (Atlas lists 11 as of Sept. 2025, not every street)
  2. Plate, image, time, and location are stored
  3. In typical Flock deployments, authorized users can type a “reason” and search (Crossville rules unpublished)
  4. You get no notice and no chance to object

TCA § 55-10-302 may limit retention windows, but it does not create a municipal immunity shield against 42 U.S.C. § 1983 claims. Crossville still chooses whether to keep buying the product.

Key precedents & what dense Flock networks look like in court

  • United States v. Jones (2012) Justice Sotomayor’s concurrence warned that GPS-style monitoring can assemble “a precise, comprehensive record of a person’s public movements” reflecting familial, political, professional, religious, and sexual associations, and that unrestrained government power to assemble that data is “susceptible to abuse.”
  • Carpenter v. United States (2018) historical cell-site location information generally requires a warrant; the Court recognized privacy in “the whole of [a person’s] physical movements.”
  • Commonwealth v. McCarthy (Mass. 2020) the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court held that the limited bridge-camera facts in that case were not a constitutional search, but warned that with enough ALPRs in enough locations, historic location data “would invade a reasonable expectation of privacy and would constitute a search.”
  • Schmidt v. Norfolk (Flock network; ongoing appellate fight), court filings and contemporaneous reporting say Norfolk’s ~176 cameras logged one plaintiff 526 times and another 849 times over roughly four months (~4× and ~6× daily), with one unnamed driver logged 14 times in about 6.5 hours, and ~200,000 database accesses citywide (NBC News). A federal judge let the case proceed in Feb. 2025 citing Carpenter-style concerns; later summary-judgment proceedings and appeal are summarized on the ACLU case page.
  • ACLU (July 2025) Flock’s Investigations Manager / Multi-State Insights / convoy and multi-location search tools are marketed to “detect patterns of suspicious activity” and alert agencies when vehicles appear across states or locations, moving from investigating a known plate toward algorithmically generating suspicion from movement patterns. East Tennessee training records documented by MaryvillePrivacy show Multi-Geo and Convoy Search in regional use, including a “got PC” example from travel history alone. Crossville should disclose whether those tools are enabled locally, see Multi-Geo & Convoy.

FAQ: Crossville Flock cameras

Do Flock / ALPR cameras actually solve much crime?

Public-records hit ratios are typically well under 1% of plate scans, and a “hit” on a wanted or stolen-vehicle list is not a cleared case. Public-records work by the Electronic Frontier Foundation and MuckRock, Spokane Flock audit math, and Atlanta FBI solved-case comparisons all undercut the sales pitch. Crossville has not published local results. Full writeup: Does this solve crime?.

Do Crossville Flock cameras require a warrant?

No. License-plate cameras scan vehicles automatically. Searching stored travel history is usually a lookup in the police system with a typed reason, not a warrant, unless the city writes stricter rules.

How many cameras does Crossville have?

The 2022 Flock quote covered 10 Falcon cameras. Atlas of Surveillance reports 11 Crossville PD Flock ALPRs as of September 2025.

Where are the cameras on a map?

As of a July 21, 2026 local check, this site has not found a city-published official public camera map. The DeFlock map shows community-reported ALPR locations around Crossville via deflock.org. Coverage may be incomplete until more residents report cameras.

What does it cost?

Quote Q-00234 shows $25,000 recurring for 10 Falcons plus $3,500 implementation (total proposal $28,500 before tax). At those rates, a 5-year horizon is about $128,500 and 10 years about $253,500 see the cost projection. Confirm current renewal pricing with finance/police budget files; Atlas reports 11 cameras, so spend may already be higher.

How do I request Crossville’s Flock records?

Use the copy/paste TPRA templates and email info@crossvilletn.gov (ATTN: City Clerk), CC’ing the City Manager. Ask for contracts, policies, audits, sharing lists, and camera inventories.

Is it legal to cover my plate to beat ALPRs in Tennessee?

No. T.C.A. § 55-4-110 prohibits tinted/camera-blocking plate covers. See the Fight back legal line and legal vehicle habits (council, TPRA, DeFlock routing).

Has Flock ALPR been misused in Tennessee?

Yes for confirmed Flock misuse: Oak Ridge officer Zachary Gauthier pleaded guilty and was sentenced after 18 personal Flock searches (among 23 official-misconduct counts), two years per count with 48 hours to serve, per local reporting. Institute for Justice reporting also says Shelby County Deputy Thadius Gordon was accused of using county ALPR databases (not confirmed here as Flock) more than 100 times to monitor an ex-wife. See Tennessee cases at a glance and the full misuse section.

Is canceling Flock enough if the city hires Axon instead?

No. Multiple cities have dropped Flock and then installed Axon plate readers to keep scanning cars: Denver, Douglas County (CO), Grand Chute (WI), Syracuse (NY), and others. That changes the company name. It does not end mass plate scanning. Other vendors shop the same job (Motorola Vigilant, Rekor, Genetec, Leonardo ELSAG / SignalTrace). Axon products raise separate concerns too (AI report-draft tools that delete drafts; an Atlanta ethics case involving an official who owned a stake in Fusus while pitching it). Do not demo, trial, or “evaluate” Axon or any other plate-reader brand. Ask council to cancel Flock, remove the cameras, and refuse any plate-reader replacement. Fix this by taking the cameras down and requiring warrants for specific lookups, not by scanning every driver. Full writeup: Not just Flock · Other vendors · Gadget Review (July 20, 2026).

Does Flock track people, or only license plates?

Flock’s core product is vehicle LPR, but FreeForm adds natural-language search that can include people descriptions (for example clothing). The company markets that cameras are not face identification. Also, Multi-Geo and Convoy Search track cross-city travel and vehicles that move together, tools that track daily travel patterns, not “only a plate hit.” Crossville should disclose whether FreeForm, Multi-Geo, and Convoy are enabled before any renewal.

What are Multi-Geo and Convoy Search?

Multi-Geo finds vehicles that appear in two or more cities inside a time window. Convoy Search flags vehicles that repeatedly show up near each other. East Tennessee training materials describe officers claiming probable cause (“got PC”) from a Detroit-Knoxville travel pattern alone. Full writeup: Multi-Geo & Convoy.

Were Flock livestreams really left open on the internet?

Yes, reporting in late 2025 documented dozens of Condor-class feeds (Flock people-tracking video cameras) discoverable via commercial search engines with no login. See the open livestream exposure section and its sources.

What are the “51 cybersecurity findings” about Flock?

Independent researcher Jon “GainSec” Gaines published a Nov. 2025 whitepaper documenting 51 findings across Flock’s public-safety device stack (22 with assigned CVEs so far), including hardcoded LPR credentials (CVE-2025-47823). Flock published a response saying many issues require physical access and that remediations continue. Full writeup: 51 cybersecurity findings.

Can Crossville trust Flock’s “immutable” audit logs?

Not as a substitute for city policy. In December 2025 Flock removed officer names, plates searched, and open-text reasons from Network Audit Logs after civilian oversight tools used those fields. Ask Crossville for city-held audit summaries, see audit-log redactions.

Has Tennessee paid a huge ALPR wrongful-arrest settlement yet?

No Tennessee ALPR settlement matching Aurora’s Gilliam case ($1.9M) or San Francisco’s Denise Green case ($495k), both non-Flock ALPR cases cited for liability scale, appears in the sources cited here. Tennessee’s record so far is felony personal use of Flock (Oak Ridge) and a reported Flock misread stop in Morristown (Herron; Business Insider names a Flock alert). Settlements elsewhere show what cities can face without written rules, see Tennessee’s record so far and liability.

What should I ask before any Flock renewal?

Ask for this first: cancel the contract and remove the cameras. If they will not cancel yet: ask council to announce a yearly hearing ahead of time and take a recorded yes-or-no vote (no automatic renewal that skips a public vote); publish monthly detailed Flock-use reports the public can check (who searched, what they searched, why, hits, and sharing); and publish a written search policy, public map, contract/sharing list, and whether FreeForm is enabled. How you can help: come to City Council and keep asking until they put it on the agenda, see What you can do, other questions worth asking, and meeting times and address.

Does Maine or Virginia have laws Tennessee could copy?

Yes. Maine broadly prohibits ALPR use except for narrow government and law-enforcement exceptions, caps ordinary data retention at 21 days, and makes violations a Class E crime. Virginia requires 21-day deletion unless tied to an active investigation, mandates written agency policies, audit trails, internal audits, public reporting, vendor restrictions, and limits on data sharing. Tennessee caps ordinary ALPR storage at 90 days under TCA § 55-10-302 but lacks that broader package. See the state-level ALPR comparison.

Does state retention law protect Crossville from lawsuits?

No. TCA § 55-10-302 addresses storage limits; it does not immunize the city from federal § 1983 liability if an ALPR-driven stop violates constitutional rights. That is a central finding in the City Council liability brief on this site.
