Tennessee has not yet had a multimillion-dollar ALPR wrongful-stop settlement like some other states. What it does have is documented felony personal use of Flock (Oak Ridge), a reported gunpoint stop after a Flock plate misread (Morristown; Business Insider archived Aug. 12, 2026), a commission rescind of existing Flock authorization (White County), a city non-renewal (Kingston), a Putnam County vendor-swap recommendation (Monterey), and a catalogued county non-renewal (Sullivan County). Those cases matter for Crossville because the same Flock system already runs here.

When oversight fails

Tennessee misuse and misread cases

Authoritarians say “if you’ve done nothing wrong, you have nothing to hide.” Tennessee’s own record shows officers do misuse these databases, sometimes against intimate partners. In the documented cases linked below, misuse was often found by victims, internal affairs, or news reporting, not by vendor marketing tools.

Open investigations and charging documents are summarized from linked sources; they are not convictions unless a plea or verdict is stated.

This is not only a Flock problem. Most documented East Tennessee cases involve Flock because that is what agencies bought. The risk is the tool itself: a searchable log of where cars go, with weak rules on who can search. Cities that “quit Flock” and install Axon (or another plate reader) keep the same problem. See Not just Flock: Axon and company swaps.
Oak Ridge, Tennessee · 2025 to 2026 · Flock misuse · sentenced

Officer Zachary Gauthier: 18 Flock searches for personal use; pleaded guilty & sentenced on 23 counts

Former Oak Ridge Police Department officer Zachary D. Gauthier accessed the Flock ALPR system 18 times for personal use between February 25 and March 25, 2025, and also misused Accurint, the State Link System, and his patrol-car computer. He was placed on leave April 21, fired April 24, indicted July 1, 2025 on 23 counts of official misconduct (Class E felony), and pleaded guilty March 23, 2026.

Per court reporting (including Crossville-area 3B Media): he received a Range I two-year sentence on each count, with 48 hours to serve and the remainder suspended to supervised probation, plus judicial diversion eligibility after probation, POST certification surrender, a mental-health assessment, no-contact conditions, and court costs.

Flock’s product supports multi-agency search across East Tennessee agencies documented by MaryvillePrivacy.org archived Aug. 14, 2026. When agencies share plate data, misuse in one city can affect drivers whose plates were scanned in another. Crossville has not published which partners can query local reads, ask before treating network membership as proven for this city.

Shelby County, Tennessee · ALPR misuse allegation

Deputy Thadius Gordon, accused of 100+ ALPR lookups of a civilian

According to Institute for Justice reporting archived Aug. 16, 2026, Shelby County Sheriff’s Office Deputy Thadius Gordon was accused of accessing county ALPR tracking databases more than 100 times to monitor a civilian without a law-enforcement purpose. He was relieved of duty and reportedly faced internal and criminal investigation. This site has not identified a final public case disposition. Primary local reporting describes SCSO plate databases; Gordon is not listed as a confirmed Flock-product case unless a primary source names Flock.

Louisville, Kentucky · 2025 · Border-state Flock stalking pattern

Former LMPD officer Roberto Cedeno, charged after alleged Flock tracking of civilians

This case is in Kentucky, not Tennessee. It shows the same kind of plate-reader / Flock access being used nearby. Former Louisville Metro Police officer Roberto Cedeno was indicted on multiple felonies (including stalking and unlawful computer access) after an internal investigation into “concerning behavior.” Institute for Justice and Courier Journal reporting describe alleged use of the city’s ALPR / Flock network to track civilians known to the officer hundreds of times over roughly two months. He resigned before termination proceedings finished.

Morristown, Tennessee · June 2025 · Flock misread / gunpoint stop · BI police-records reporting

Morristown family stopped at gunpoint after a Flock plate misread

According to Business Insider archived Aug. 12, 2026 reporting on police records, Morristown officers received a Flock alert after a plate was matched to a stolen plate. Guns were drawn and adults were handcuffed until the mismatch was sorted out. BI also describes a Flock investigative summary provided to officers that included a photo of the stopped vehicle.

Local WATE coverage archived June 12, 2025 (Yahoo News republish) describes the same gunpoint stop; that account focuses on a stolen-tag / crime-information-database mismatch and does not name the ALPR vendor. Morristown separately approved Flock LPRs in Sept. 2024 local reporting, so the city runs Flock, and BI’s police-records reporting is what names Flock for this specific stop.

Sources: Business Insider: Flock misreads (names Flock alert / investigative summary) archived Aug. 12, 2026 · WATE / Yahoo: Morristown gunpoint stop (local account) archived June 12, 2025 · Institute for Justice: ALPR error detentions archived Aug. 15, 2026 · WBIR: Morristown approves Flock LPRs · wrongful-enforcement ID 31 in the wrongful-stop database.

What Tennessee has, and has not, shown so far: Tennessee has not yet paid a settlement like Aurora’s Gilliam case ($1.9M) or San Francisco’s Denise Green case ($495k), both are documented ALPR wrongful-stop cases, not Flock-branded products. Tennessee has documented felony personal use of Flock in Oak Ridge, a reported Flock misread gunpoint stop in Morristown (Business Insider archived Aug. 12, 2026 police-records reporting), and a nearby alleged stalking pattern in Louisville involving ALPR/Flock access. Large ALPR settlements elsewhere show what cities can face when plate-reader programs run without clear rules.

State law

State-level ALPR action: a comparison

What other states have done, and what Tennessee hasn’t. Federal court rules set a minimum. States can pass stronger privacy laws on plate-reader surveillance. Maine and Virginia have done so. Tennessee has not. The table below summarizes the contrast.

Comparison adapted from the East Tennessee Regional ALPR Transparency Center archived Aug. 16, 2026 (Maryville Residents for Privacy).

Protection Maine Virginia Tennessee
Broad prohibition on ALPR use YES (with narrow government / LE exceptions) No No
Data retention cap (ordinary captures) 21 days 21 days (unless tied to active investigation) 90-day max under TCA § 55-10-302 (investigation exceptions); Flock default often 30 days, not a Maine/Virginia-style package
Written agency policies required Yes Yes No
Audit trail and internal audits required Yes Yes No
Mandatory public reporting Yes Yes No
Vendor practice restrictions Yes Yes No
Restrictions on out-of-state data sharing Yes Yes No
Penalty for violation Class E crime Statutory civil and administrative remedies None

What this means for Tennessee residents: the stronger Maine and Virginia protections, shorter ordinary retention (21 days), mandatory policies/audits/public reporting, vendor restrictions, and real penalties, are things the Tennessee General Assembly could still enact. If lawmakers refuse those upgrades, Tennessee keeps weaker protections than Maine or Virginia. That is the legislation Cumberland County’s representatives will vote on, oppose, or sponsor.

Tennessee’s retention row above reflects TCA § 55-10-302 archived May 5, 2025 (generally no more than 90 days unless retained for an ongoing investigation). That caps how long data may be stored; it is not Maine/Virginia’s protective package: it does not create a broad use prohibition, mandatory written policies, audit trails, public reporting, vendor restrictions, out-of-state sharing limits, or a penalty scheme like the other rows. See also the PEEPS Act effort under Tennessee politician positions.

Primary statutes: Maine Title 29-A § 2117-A archived Aug. 14, 2026 · Virginia Code § 2.2-5517 archived Aug. 16, 2026 · TCA § 55-10-302 archived May 5, 2025.

Regional network

East Tennessee ALPR network: Crossville’s place in it

Crossville’s cameras do not operate in a vacuum. Flock’s product is built for multi-agency search: plate reads from one city can become searchable far beyond Cumberland County where sharing is enabled. Oak Ridge has a documented Flock personal-use conviction; Crossville has not published which partners can query local reads. Next door, White County rescinded Flock authorization. On I-40 east, Monterey’s Law Committee recommended swapping most Flock cameras for Axon while keeping one Flock for the national share. Sullivan County’s commission voted 18–0 not to renew after the sheriff suspended that office’s 10 cameras. Loudon County’s sheriff held a public meeting on a proposed countywide Flock rollout; WVLT archived Aug. 19, 2026 reported the room opposed it and no decision was made. Johnson City’s 2025 Safe City deal shows how fast a regional camera network can grow. Middle Tennessee is in the same fight: FOX 17’s July 2026 debate piece covers Lebanon residents asking council to cancel and Cheatham County expanding past 20 cameras.

Loudon County residents oppose a proposed Flock rollout; sheriff seeks more input (Aug. 19, 2026)

WVLT archived Aug. 19, 2026 reported August 19, 2026 that Loudon County Sheriff Jimmy Davis held a public meeting Monday night on a proposed countywide Flock license-plate-reader rollout. A moderator asked who wanted the cameras; WVLT said no one responded. When asked who did not want them, the entire room raised their hands. No decision was made. Davis said he wants more input from residents first.

Davis called the cameras an important law-enforcement tool and said his office is looking at guardrails. WVLT quoted him saying he can cite crimes solved, “but it really doesn’t matter if it violates the rights.” Assistant Chief Deputy Chris Hutchens said, “I think the worst statement in the world is if you have nothing to hide you shouldn’t be worried about it.” The piece notes similar Flock debates in Knoxville, Knox County, and Clinton. This is a proposed rollout. Coverage does not report a signed contract, a camera count, a cancel, or a pause.

Monterey Law Committee recommends swapping most Flock cameras for Axon (Aug. 17, 2026)

The Herald-Citizen archived Aug. 21, 2026 reported that Monterey’s Law Committee voted Monday night, August 17, 2026, to recommend replacing most of the town’s Flock cameras with Axon readers. Putnam County sits on I-40 east of Cumberland County. Lt. Andrew Neff said Flock’s cost is going up and that Flock data “can be shared everywhere pretty much,” while Axon stores data in-house. Search rules, he said, are similar: officers still have to enter a reason and a case or CAD number.

The department wants to keep one Flock camera so Monterey stays on the nationwide share. The Board of Mayor and Aldermen has not finally approved the change. A related Herald-Citizen archived Aug. 18, 2026 Cookeville piece notes the same Monterey proposal. This is not a privacy win. Replacing Flock with Axon keeps warrantless scanning under a new logo, and they still want one Flock camera on the national share. That is the vendor-swap risk on this site’s Not just Flock section.

Kingston will not renew two Flock cameras when the trial expires in September (Aug. 19, 2026)

The Mountain Press / Roane County News archived Aug. 21, 2026 reported August 19, 2026 that Kingston will not renew its Flock Safety contract. City Manager David Bolling said the two cameras were a trial and that the benefit did not justify the cost for a small department. Mayor Tim Neal said the decision is financial: about $6,000 a year. The 2024 deal was $12,300 for two years and expires in September.

Cameras stay up until that expiration. Neal said the choice is not about the national privacy fight.

White County rescinds Flock authorization; 12 cameras may still be standing (July 20, 2026)

UCNOW archived Aug. 21, 2026 reported that the White County Commission voted July 20, 2026 to rescind the April 2025 resolution that authorized Flock cameras. Chair Derrick Hutchings said the board reversed after resident privacy pushback. He said the sheriff’s office asked for the cameras, that units went up around September or October 2025, and that they likely still collect data while they stand. Removal, he said, sits with the sheriff’s office because that agency started the contract. County Attorney Sam Benningfield said the county should not touch vendor equipment if it is not county property, and that the state had given permission for the right-of-way.

In June the commission had already stripped about $48,000 meant for Flock while leaving the dollars in the sheriff’s budget, UCNOW archived Aug. 21, 2026 reported. That coverage counted 12 county cameras; Sparta’s cameras are a separate city program. On June 2 the budget committee had kept camera funding 3–1, with County Executive Denny Wayne Robinson the no vote, per a third UCNOW archived June 7, 2026 report.

After the June funding strip, Sparta Live archived July 18, 2026 quoted Sheriff Steve Page saying he told Flock the county cameras were unfunded and could come down; pickup still waits on the vendor. That is not a confirmed hardware pull. Sparta city cameras stay.

Maryville’s pro-Flock police chief loses House District 8 primary in a landslide (Aug. 6, 2026)

Maryville Police Chief Tony Jay Crisp lost the August 6, 2026 Republican primary for Tennessee House District 8 in a landslide. Teacher Jordan Henderson beat him 5,873–3,540 (~62%–38%), per the Daily Times archived Aug. 8, 2026. Henderson advances to November against Democrat Hilary Trudell.

Money did not save him. Campaign-finance reporting cited by Tennessee Firefly archived Aug. 16, 2026 put Crisp at about $82,280 in contributions versus Henderson’s about $12,600, roughly a 6.5-to-1 cash advantage for the chief. He still lost by about 25 points.

MaryvillePrivacy.org archived Aug. 16, 2026 published Tennessee Public Records Act materials on Crisp’s stewardship of Maryville’s Flock program and his ongoing relationship with Flock sales contact Alex Guth. Their dossier documents vendor emails and hospitality, multi-jurisdiction drone pricing pitched across Maryville PD, Alcoa PD, Blount County Sheriff’s Office, and Blount 911, and a February–March 2025 thread in which Guth asked Crisp to introduce him to the Maryville Housing Authority so a HUD grant could fund Flock products. Crisp agreed to make the introduction. MaryvillePrivacy’s writeup calls that pattern using the chief as a sales facilitator for additional Flock sales. Crossville should refuse that playbook.

Nashville Banner: Tennessee politicians split further on Flock and LPRs (Aug. 12, 2026)

The Nashville Banner archived Aug. 13, 2026 reported August 12 that opposition to license-plate readers is spreading among Tennessee politicians even as Flock still has four lobbyists registered at the statehouse. The piece quotes Mayor Glenn Jacobs telling the Banner the cameras are not “old school license plate readers,” that Tennessee’s LPR statutes date to 2014 with a minor 2021 update, and that “technology has gotten way far ahead.” Senate Majority Leader Jack Johnson (R-Franklin) said he is “very concerned about the advancement of AI” and that when security and liberty are in tension, “we have to fall on the side of liberty.” Democratic gubernatorial nominee Jerri Green wrote that unregulated mass surveillance threatens constitutional privacy and called for non-negotiable guardrails, including a ban on sharing local data with federal immigration authorities. Republican nominee Marsha Blackburn answered a Banner questionnaire “Yes, I do” support LPRs and Flock cameras, then struck a more careful tone standing with Jacobs and Johnson, drawing a line between reading a plate and “24-hour surveillance.”

In the 2nd Congressional District, Democrat Michaela Barnett agrees with Rep. Tim Burchett that the cameras are mass surveillance. TDOT told the Banner no license agreements have been issued to the Tennessee Highway Patrol for LPRs on state right-of-way. Nashville’s citywide LPR plan remains stalled.

Sullivan County Commission votes 18–0 not to renew Flock (Aug. 20, 2026)

WJHL (via Yahoo) archived Aug. 21, 2026 reported that the Sullivan County Commission voted 18–0 on August 20, 2026 not to renew the county’s Flock contract. The resolution was amended to say Flock cameras would not be used within the county. It does not apply to Kingsport or Bristol police cameras. The contract ends in January 2027.

Three days earlier, WVLT archived Aug. 18, 2026 reported that Sheriff Jeffrey Cassidy suspended the sheriff’s office’s use of Flock until further notice. Cassidy cited privacy, oversight, and government-surveillance concerns. WVLT says the office runs 10 Flock cameras, all on state highways. He asked the commission to consider a referendum; elections administrator Jason Booher told WJHL the commission cannot put that on the ballot. Nearby and private Flock can continue.

Sheriff use is already off. County cameras stay in the contract until January 2027. White County, Kingston, and Monterey are covered separately.
