Documented Tennessee cases

Flock’s product supports multi-agency search; Crossville has not published its partner list. Below: documented Flock misuse (Oak Ridge), a reported ALPR allegation (Shelby), a Flock misread stop reported by Business Insider (Morristown), and a large nearby deployment (Johnson City).

Tennessee has not yet had a multimillion-dollar ALPR wrongful-stop settlement like some other states. What it does have is documented felony personal use of Flock (Oak Ridge) and a reported gunpoint stop after a Flock plate misread (Morristown; Business Insider). Those cases matter for Crossville because the same Flock system already runs here.

When oversight fails

Tennessee misuse and misread cases

Authoritarians say “if you’ve done nothing wrong, you have nothing to hide.” Tennessee’s own record shows officers do misuse these databases, sometimes against intimate partners. In the documented cases linked below, misuse was often found by victims, internal affairs, or news reporting, not by vendor marketing tools.

Reading this section: Where someone was charged, pleaded, or sentenced, we say so and link sources. Where reporting describes an accusation or investigation without a final public disposition, we label it as an allegation or as attributed reporting, not as a finding of guilt by this site.

This is not only a Flock problem. Most documented East Tennessee cases involve Flock because that is what agencies bought. The risk is the tool itself: a searchable log of where cars go, with weak rules on who can search. Cities that “quit Flock” and install Axon (or another plate reader) keep the same problem. See Not just Flock: Axon and company swaps.
Oak Ridge, Tennessee · 2025 to 2026 · Flock misuse · sentenced

Officer Zachary Gauthier: 18 Flock searches for personal use; pleaded guilty & sentenced on 23 counts

Former Oak Ridge Police Department officer Zachary D. Gauthier accessed the Flock ALPR system 18 times for personal use between February 25 and March 25, 2025, and also misused Accurint, the State Link System, and his patrol-car computer. He was placed on leave April 21, fired April 24, indicted July 1, 2025 on 23 counts of official misconduct (Class E felony), and pleaded guilty March 23, 2026.

Per court reporting (including Crossville-area 3B Media): he received a Range I two-year sentence on each count, with 48 hours to serve and the remainder suspended to supervised probation, plus judicial diversion eligibility after probation, POST certification surrender, a mental-health assessment, no-contact conditions, and court costs.

Flock’s product supports multi-agency search across East Tennessee agencies documented by MaryvillePrivacy.org. When agencies share plate data, misuse in one city can affect drivers whose plates were scanned in another. Crossville has not published which partners can query local reads, ask before treating network membership as proven for this city.

Sources: Maryville Residents for Privacy · 3B Media (sentence) · WATE / Yahoo (plea & sentence) · WBIR (indictment) · WBIR (plea) · WATE (indictment) · WVLT (indictment) · WVLT (plea) · Catalogued as FLK-2025-027 in the misuse master database.

Shelby County, Tennessee · ALPR misuse allegation

Deputy Thadius Gordon, accused of 100+ ALPR lookups of an ex-wife

According to Institute for Justice reporting, Shelby County Sheriff’s Office Deputy Thadius Gordon was accused of accessing county ALPR tracking databases more than 100 times to monitor his ex-wife without a law-enforcement purpose. He was relieved of duty and reportedly faced internal and criminal investigation. This site has not identified a final public case disposition. Primary local reporting describes SCSO plate databases; this site does not treat Gordon as a confirmed Flock-product case unless a primary source names Flock.

Source: Institute for Justice: ALPR stalking report · Catalogued as ALPR-2024-030 (not a confirmed Flock product case) in the misuse master database.

Louisville, Kentucky · 2025 · Border-state Flock stalking pattern

Former LMPD officer Roberto Cedeno, charged after alleged Flock tracking of an ex and friends

This case is in Kentucky, not Tennessee. It shows the same kind of plate-reader / Flock access being used nearby. Former Louisville Metro Police officer Roberto Cedeno was indicted on multiple felonies (including stalking and unlawful computer access) after an internal investigation into “concerning behavior.” Institute for Justice and Courier Journal reporting describe alleged use of the city’s ALPR / Flock network to track an ex-partner and her friends hundreds of times over roughly two months. He resigned before termination proceedings finished.

Sources: Institute for Justice: ALPR stalking catalog (Cedeno) · Courier Journal: Cedeno charges / arrest · Courier Journal: Louisville Flock map / Cedeno IA files · WHAS11

Morristown, Tennessee · June 2025 · Flock misread / gunpoint stop · BI police-records reporting

Herron family, personalized plate LOVEY misread as stolen L0VEY

According to Business Insider reporting on police records, Morristown officers received a Flock alert on JC and Carolyn Herron’s nurse plate LOVEY (letter O), matched to a stolen Ford F-150 plate L0VEY (zero). Guns were drawn; adults were handcuffed; their 3-year-old granddaughter was left alone in the vehicle until the mismatch was sorted out. BI also describes a Flock investigative summary provided to officers that included a photo of the Herrons’ Expedition with the plate recorded using a zero.

Local WATE coverage (Yahoo News republish) describes the same gunpoint stop and O/0 mix-up; that account focuses on the stolen-tag / crime-information-database mismatch and does not name the ALPR vendor. Morristown separately approved Flock LPRs in Sept. 2024 local reporting, so the city runs Flock, and BI’s police-records reporting is what names Flock for this specific stop.

Sources: Business Insider: Flock misreads (names Flock alert / investigative summary) · WATE / Yahoo: Herron stop (local account) · Institute for Justice: ALPR error detentions · WBIR: Morristown approves Flock LPRs · Also summarized in the City Council liability brief.

What Tennessee has, and has not, shown so far: Tennessee has not yet paid a settlement like Aurora’s Gilliam case ($1.9M) or San Francisco’s Denise Green case ($495k), both are documented ALPR wrongful-stop cases, not Flock-branded products. Tennessee has documented felony personal use of Flock in Oak Ridge, a reported Flock misread gunpoint stop in Morristown (Business Insider police-records reporting), and a nearby alleged stalking pattern in Louisville involving ALPR/Flock access. Large ALPR settlements elsewhere show what cities can face when plate-reader programs run without clear rules.

  • Rep. Tim Burchett U.S. House, Tennessee 2nd District (East TN)
    Introduced H.R. 9800, the federal Protection Against Mass Surveillance Act (July 21, 2026), to ban federal agencies from purchasing, deploying, operating, accessing, or contracting for automated license-plate systems including a Flock Safety camera, facial recognition, other biometrics, and persistent mass-surveillance imaging, and to block state/local/Tribal use of federal funds for the same tools. Improperly obtained data would have to be deleted and would be inadmissible. Burchett called the cameras “unconstitutional”: “We gotta ban these dadgum things.” August 1 coverage said Flock argued the bill would effectively ban the cameras and called instead for federal “safeguards.” In an August 3 WATE interview he framed the bill as blocking federal spend while leaving local choice: “All it says is that there will be no federal money spent on Flock cameras. If the locals want to do it, they do it.” Companion pressure: Rep. Massie’s separate funding-withhold tease on the national page. State law

State-level ALPR action: a comparison

What other states have done, and what Tennessee hasn’t. Federal court rules set a minimum. States can pass stronger privacy laws on plate-reader surveillance. Maine and Virginia have done so. Tennessee has not. The table below summarizes the contrast.

Comparison adapted from the East Tennessee Regional ALPR Transparency Center (Maryville Residents for Privacy).

Protection Maine Virginia Tennessee
Broad prohibition on ALPR use YES (with narrow government / LE exceptions) No No
Data retention cap (ordinary captures) 21 days 21 days (unless tied to active investigation) 90-day max under TCA § 55-10-302 (investigation exceptions); Flock default often 30 days, not a Maine/Virginia-style package
Written agency policies required Yes Yes No
Audit trail and internal audits required Yes Yes No
Mandatory public reporting Yes Yes No
Vendor practice restrictions Yes Yes No
Restrictions on out-of-state data sharing Yes Yes No
Penalty for violation Class E crime Statutory civil and administrative remedies None

What this means for Tennessee residents: the stronger Maine and Virginia protections, shorter ordinary retention (21 days), mandatory policies/audits/public reporting, vendor restrictions, and real penalties, are things the Tennessee General Assembly could still enact. If lawmakers refuse those upgrades, Tennessee keeps weaker protections than Maine or Virginia. That is the legislation Cumberland County’s representatives will vote on, oppose, or sponsor.

Tennessee’s retention row above reflects TCA § 55-10-302 (generally no more than 90 days unless retained for an ongoing investigation). That caps how long data may be stored; it is not Maine/Virginia’s protective package: it does not create a broad use prohibition, mandatory written policies, audit trails, public reporting, vendor restrictions, out-of-state sharing limits, or a penalty scheme like the other rows. See also the PEEPS Act effort under Tennessee politician positions.

Primary statutes: Maine Title 29-A § 2117-A · Virginia Code § 2.2-5517 · TCA § 55-10-302.

Regional network

East Tennessee ALPR network: Crossville’s place in it

Crossville’s cameras do not operate in a vacuum. Flock’s product is built for multi-agency search: plate reads from one city can become searchable far beyond Cumberland County where sharing is enabled. Oak Ridge has a documented Flock personal-use conviction; Crossville has not published which partners can query local reads. Johnson City’s 2025 Safe City deal shows how fast a regional camera network can grow. Middle Tennessee is in the same fight: FOX 17’s July 2026 debate piece covers Lebanon residents asking council to cancel and Cheatham County expanding past 20 cameras.

Middle Tennessee debate: Lebanon cancel push vs Cheatham expansion (FOX 17)

On July 28, 2026, FOX 17 / WZTV reported the statewide tension: Lebanon residents (including Mikal Qualls and Rick Julian) have urged City Council to dissolve the Flock contract over searchable travel histories and Fourth Amendment concerns, while Cheatham County Sheriff’s Office Capt. Chris Gilmore defended more than 20 county cameras with investigative examples and said data is retained for deputies for 30 days, without facial recognition or seeing who is driving. The piece also notes Rep. Tim Burchett’s federal Protection Against Mass Surveillance Act and the DeFlock mapping project.

The same Flock system Crossville uses. The same arguments at council. Crossville should cancel and remove cameras, not wait for Middle Tennessee to settle the debate first.

Sources: FOX 17 / WZTV: Tennessee debates Flock cameras (July 28, 2026) · YouTube segment · Media archive

Knox County: $800k year-one PO, then “year 1 of 10” without full cost disclosure (July 31, 2026)

Knox News reports it obtained a Knox County Sheriff’s Office purchase order for year one of a Flock Safety deal at $800,000 for a “Flock Safety Bundle and Flock Safety Professional Services.” KCSO later asked procurement to revise the order from a one-year buy to year one of ten. Procurement Director Mandi Benedict told Knox News her office has not been part of discussions about costs or details for years two through ten. The PO request did not include a service contract, only a Flock order form; KCSO used a cooperative / piggyback purchase (Houston-area Region 4 ESC / OMNIA-style contract path) so the equipment buy did not go through a mayor/commission service-contract vote. Cameras were purchased around April 20; Knox News says mayor and commissioners were not told about the planned surveillance hub until July 6. Some cameras were already installed in East Knox County. Future service contracts over $100,000 still need commission/mayor buy-in, so the pause fight continues into the next sheriff’s term. Crossville lesson: ask for the full multi-year cost, the written service terms, and a recorded public vote before any renewal, not just a first-year invoice. August 4 reporting on where those cameras actually went: placement outside the grant’s high-crime zip codes.

Sources: Knox News: 10-year deal / $800k PO (July 31, 2026) · OMNIA Partners: Flock contract R250203 documents · Placement / high-crime zones · Pause / Commission update · Politicians

Knox County: cameras installed outside the high-crime zones KCSO promised (Aug. 4, 2026)

Knox News reported that KCSO’s state-grant plan named six Knoxville-area zip codes with the highest crime rates (37918, 37917, 37914, 37915, 37920, 37921) and said the agency wanted to avoid overlapping Knoxville PD’s camera network. A month before the Advanced Research Center hub was set to launch, cameras were also going up in rural districts such as Corryton and Mascot, far outside those zip codes. Procurement director Hugh Holt told Knox News the office is putting cameras in the targeted zones and throughout the county, and that “We do not own the hardware; we own the data that it produces.” Commissioner Adam Thompson, who represents Corryton and Mascot, has led the pushback after spotting cameras in his district. County engineering stopped new right-of-way installs pending mayor/commission direction. New cameras remain offline through Spangler’s term (Sept. 1); Commission is set to vote Aug. 31 on a potential ban, after the Aug. 24 workshop. KCSO still had not published a data policy or full public location list as of that reporting.

Sources: Knox News: cameras outside high-crime zones (Aug. 4, 2026) · $800k / 10-year PO · Aug. 24 workshop · Pause / Commission update

Knox County: Aug. 24 workshop with Flock reps (no public comment)

After the cancelled July workshop, Knox County scheduled a public workshop for Monday, August 24, 2026 at 1:00 p.m. in the main assembly room of the City-County Building (400 Main Street, Knoxville). Knox County Commission, the Sheriff’s Office, and Flock personnel are expected to discuss Flock cameras and the proposed Advanced Research and Communications (ARC) Center. Coverage says the meeting is open to the public but will not include a public forum and that no votes will be taken. New installs remain paused; existing LPRs stay online. Crossville residents watching East TN: this is the next on-the-record chance to see how KCSO answers privacy, access, audit, and sharing questions before the next sheriff takes office Sept. 1. Related: Aug. 4 placement reporting and a possible Commission ban vote on Aug. 31.

Sources: WVLT: Aug. 24 workshop set (July 29, 2026) · WATE: public workshop details · Placement / high-crime zones · $800k / 10-year PO · Pause / Commission update

Knox County, expansion paused; Commission rejects immediate ban (late July 2026)

After Mayor Glenn Jacobs and commissioners challenged a grant-funded plan for roughly 270-300 additional Flock cameras / ARC capacity, Sheriff Tom Spangler said remaining installs were paused while existing LPRs (reporting cites about 84) stay online with ~30-day retention. Jacobs and Spangler agreed new cameras would stay offline through the end of their terms (Sept. 1). On July 28, Knox County Commission continued the debate into August, demanded written policies on privacy, access, audits, and sharing, and rejected an emergency ban. Incoming elected officials after Sept. 1 inherit the fight. A rescheduled workshop with Flock is set for Aug. 24. Separate July 31 reporting documents an $800,000 year-one purchase order later framed as year one of ten: read that update. State Rep. Jason Zachary and Rep. Tim Burchett are pushing state/federal limits; same coverage notes Knoxville PD runs Axon, not Flock.
