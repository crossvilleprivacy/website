Tennessee has not yet had a multimillion-dollar ALPR wrongful-stop settlement like some other states. What it does have is documented felony personal use of Flock (Oak Ridge), a reported gunpoint stop after a Flock plate misread (Morristown; Business Insider), a commission rescind of existing Flock authorization (White County), a city non-renewal (Kingston), and a catalogued sheriff suspend (Sullivan County). Those cases matter for Crossville because the same Flock system already runs here.

When oversight fails

Tennessee misuse and misread cases

Authoritarians say “if you’ve done nothing wrong, you have nothing to hide.” Tennessee’s own record shows officers do misuse these databases, sometimes against intimate partners. In the documented cases linked below, misuse was often found by victims, internal affairs, or news reporting, not by vendor marketing tools.

Open investigations and charging documents are summarized from linked sources; they are not convictions unless a plea or verdict is stated.

This is not only a Flock problem. Most documented East Tennessee cases involve Flock because that is what agencies bought. The risk is the tool itself: a searchable log of where cars go, with weak rules on who can search. Cities that “quit Flock” and install Axon (or another plate reader) keep the same problem. See Not just Flock: Axon and company swaps.
Oak Ridge, Tennessee · 2025 to 2026 · Flock misuse · sentenced

Officer Zachary Gauthier: 18 Flock searches for personal use; pleaded guilty & sentenced on 23 counts

Former Oak Ridge Police Department officer Zachary D. Gauthier accessed the Flock ALPR system 18 times for personal use between February 25 and March 25, 2025, and also misused Accurint, the State Link System, and his patrol-car computer. He was placed on leave April 21, fired April 24, indicted July 1, 2025 on 23 counts of official misconduct (Class E felony), and pleaded guilty March 23, 2026.

Per court reporting (including Crossville-area 3B Media): he received a Range I two-year sentence on each count, with 48 hours to serve and the remainder suspended to supervised probation, plus judicial diversion eligibility after probation, POST certification surrender, a mental-health assessment, no-contact conditions, and court costs.

Flock’s product supports multi-agency search across East Tennessee agencies documented by MaryvillePrivacy.org. When agencies share plate data, misuse in one city can affect drivers whose plates were scanned in another. Crossville has not published which partners can query local reads, ask before treating network membership as proven for this city.

Sources: Maryville Residents for Privacy · 3B Media (sentence) · WATE / Yahoo (plea & sentence) · WBIR (indictment) · WBIR (plea) · WATE (indictment) · WVLT (indictment) · WVLT (plea) · Catalogued as FLK-2025-027 in the misuse master database.

Shelby County, Tennessee · ALPR misuse allegation

Deputy Thadius Gordon, accused of 100+ ALPR lookups of a civilian

According to Institute for Justice reporting, Shelby County Sheriff’s Office Deputy Thadius Gordon was accused of accessing county ALPR tracking databases more than 100 times to monitor a civilian without a law-enforcement purpose. He was relieved of duty and reportedly faced internal and criminal investigation. This site has not identified a final public case disposition. Primary local reporting describes SCSO plate databases; Gordon is not listed as a confirmed Flock-product case unless a primary source names Flock.

Source: Institute for Justice: ALPR stalking report · Catalogued as ALPR-2024-030 (not a confirmed Flock product case) in the misuse master database.

Louisville, Kentucky · 2025 · Border-state Flock stalking pattern

Former LMPD officer Roberto Cedeno, charged after alleged Flock tracking of civilians

This case is in Kentucky, not Tennessee. It shows the same kind of plate-reader / Flock access being used nearby. Former Louisville Metro Police officer Roberto Cedeno was indicted on multiple felonies (including stalking and unlawful computer access) after an internal investigation into “concerning behavior.” Institute for Justice and Courier Journal reporting describe alleged use of the city’s ALPR / Flock network to track civilians known to the officer hundreds of times over roughly two months. He resigned before termination proceedings finished.

Sources: Institute for Justice: ALPR stalking catalog (Cedeno) · Courier Journal: Cedeno charges / arrest · Courier Journal: Louisville Flock map / Cedeno IA files · WHAS11 · FLK-2025-023 in the misuse database.

Morristown, Tennessee · June 2025 · Flock misread / gunpoint stop · BI police-records reporting

Morristown family stopped at gunpoint after a Flock plate misread

According to Business Insider reporting on police records, Morristown officers received a Flock alert after a plate was matched to a stolen plate. Guns were drawn and adults were handcuffed until the mismatch was sorted out. BI also describes a Flock investigative summary provided to officers that included a photo of the stopped vehicle.

Local WATE coverage (Yahoo News republish) describes the same gunpoint stop; that account focuses on a stolen-tag / crime-information-database mismatch and does not name the ALPR vendor. Morristown separately approved Flock LPRs in Sept. 2024 local reporting, so the city runs Flock, and BI’s police-records reporting is what names Flock for this specific stop.

Sources: Business Insider: Flock misreads (names Flock alert / investigative summary) · WATE / Yahoo: Morristown gunpoint stop (local account) · Institute for Justice: ALPR error detentions · WBIR: Morristown approves Flock LPRs · wrongful-enforcement ID 31 in the wrongful-stop database.

What Tennessee has, and has not, shown so far: Tennessee has not yet paid a settlement like Aurora’s Gilliam case ($1.9M) or San Francisco’s Denise Green case ($495k), both are documented ALPR wrongful-stop cases, not Flock-branded products. Tennessee has documented felony personal use of Flock in Oak Ridge, a reported Flock misread gunpoint stop in Morristown (Business Insider police-records reporting), and a nearby alleged stalking pattern in Louisville involving ALPR/Flock access. Large ALPR settlements elsewhere show what cities can face when plate-reader programs run without clear rules.

State law

State-level ALPR action: a comparison

What other states have done, and what Tennessee hasn’t. Federal court rules set a minimum. States can pass stronger privacy laws on plate-reader surveillance. Maine and Virginia have done so. Tennessee has not. The table below summarizes the contrast.

Comparison adapted from the East Tennessee Regional ALPR Transparency Center (Maryville Residents for Privacy).

Protection Maine Virginia Tennessee
Broad prohibition on ALPR use YES (with narrow government / LE exceptions) No No
Data retention cap (ordinary captures) 21 days 21 days (unless tied to active investigation) 90-day max under TCA § 55-10-302 (investigation exceptions); Flock default often 30 days, not a Maine/Virginia-style package
Written agency policies required Yes Yes No
Audit trail and internal audits required Yes Yes No
Mandatory public reporting Yes Yes No
Vendor practice restrictions Yes Yes No
Restrictions on out-of-state data sharing Yes Yes No
Penalty for violation Class E crime Statutory civil and administrative remedies None

What this means for Tennessee residents: the stronger Maine and Virginia protections, shorter ordinary retention (21 days), mandatory policies/audits/public reporting, vendor restrictions, and real penalties, are things the Tennessee General Assembly could still enact. If lawmakers refuse those upgrades, Tennessee keeps weaker protections than Maine or Virginia. That is the legislation Cumberland County’s representatives will vote on, oppose, or sponsor.

Tennessee’s retention row above reflects TCA § 55-10-302 (generally no more than 90 days unless retained for an ongoing investigation). That caps how long data may be stored; it is not Maine/Virginia’s protective package: it does not create a broad use prohibition, mandatory written policies, audit trails, public reporting, vendor restrictions, out-of-state sharing limits, or a penalty scheme like the other rows. See also the PEEPS Act effort under Tennessee politician positions.

Primary statutes: Maine Title 29-A § 2117-A · Virginia Code § 2.2-5517 · TCA § 55-10-302.

Regional network

East Tennessee ALPR network: Crossville’s place in it

Crossville’s cameras do not operate in a vacuum. Flock’s product is built for multi-agency search: plate reads from one city can become searchable far beyond Cumberland County where sharing is enabled. Oak Ridge has a documented Flock personal-use conviction; Crossville has not published which partners can query local reads. Sullivan County’s sheriff has now suspended that office’s 10 Flock cameras until further notice and asked for a public vote. Loudon County’s sheriff held a public meeting on a proposed countywide Flock rollout; WVLT reported the room opposed it and no decision was made. Johnson City’s 2025 Safe City deal shows how fast a regional camera network can grow. Middle Tennessee is in the same fight: FOX 17’s July 2026 debate piece covers Lebanon residents asking council to cancel and Cheatham County expanding past 20 cameras.

Loudon County residents oppose a proposed Flock rollout; sheriff seeks more input (Aug. 19, 2026)

WVLT reported August 19, 2026 that Loudon County Sheriff Jimmy Davis held a public meeting Monday night on a proposed countywide Flock license-plate-reader rollout. A moderator asked who wanted the cameras; WVLT said no one responded. When asked who did not want them, the entire room raised their hands. No decision was made. Davis said he wants more input from residents first.

Davis called the cameras an important law-enforcement tool and said his office is looking at guardrails. WVLT quoted him saying he can cite crimes solved, “but it really doesn’t matter if it violates the rights.” Assistant Chief Deputy Chris Hutchens said, “I think the worst statement in the world is if you have nothing to hide you shouldn’t be worried about it.” The piece notes similar Flock debates in Knoxville, Knox County, and Clinton. This is a proposed rollout. Coverage does not report a signed contract, a camera count, a cancel, or a pause.

Sources: WVLT: Loudon County residents push back on Flock camera proposal (Aug. 19, 2026)

Kingston will not renew two Flock cameras when the trial expires in September (Aug. 19, 2026)

The Mountain Press / Roane County News reported August 19, 2026 that Kingston will not renew its Flock Safety contract. City Manager David Bolling said the two cameras were a trial and that the benefit did not justify the cost for a small department. Mayor Tim Neal said the decision is financial: about $6,000 a year. The 2024 deal was $12,300 for two years and expires in September.

This is a catalogued Tennessee non-renewal (National card; CAN-2026-114). Cameras stay up until that expiration. Neal said the choice is not about the national privacy fight.

Sources: Mountain Press: Kingston not renewing Flock (Aug. 19, 2026) · National writeup · Cancellations table

White County rescinds Flock authorization; 12 cameras may still be standing (July 20, 2026)

UCNOW reported that the White County Commission voted July 20, 2026 to rescind the April 2025 resolution that authorized Flock cameras. Chair Derrick Hutchings said the board reversed after resident privacy pushback. He said the sheriff’s office asked for the cameras, that units went up around September or October 2025, and that they likely still collect data while they stand. Removal, he said, sits with the sheriff’s office because that agency started the contract. County Attorney Sam Benningfield said the county should not touch vendor equipment if it is not county property, and that the state had given permission for the right-of-way.

In June the commission had already stripped about $48,000 meant for Flock while leaving the dollars in the sheriff’s budget, UCNOW reported. That coverage counted 12 county cameras; Sparta’s cameras are a separate city program. On June 2 the budget committee had kept camera funding 3–1, with County Executive Denny Wayne Robinson the no vote, per a third UCNOW report.

This is a catalogued Tennessee rescind (National card; CAN-2026-113). It is not a confirmed hardware pull.

Maryville’s pro-Flock police chief loses House District 8 primary in a landslide (Aug. 6, 2026)

Maryville Police Chief Tony Jay Crisp lost the August 6, 2026 Republican primary for Tennessee House District 8 in a landslide. Teacher Jordan Henderson beat him 5,873–3,540 (~62%–38%), per the Daily Times. Henderson advances to November against Democrat Hilary Trudell.

Money did not save him. Campaign-finance reporting cited by Tennessee Firefly put Crisp at about $82,280 in contributions versus Henderson’s about $12,600, roughly a 6.5-to-1 cash advantage for the chief. He still lost by about 25 points.

MaryvillePrivacy.org published Tennessee Public Records Act materials on Crisp’s stewardship of Maryville’s Flock program and his ongoing relationship with Flock sales contact Alex Guth. Their dossier documents vendor emails and hospitality, multi-jurisdiction drone pricing pitched across Maryville PD, Alcoa PD, Blount County Sheriff’s Office, and Blount 911, and a February–March 2025 thread in which Guth asked Crisp to introduce him to the Maryville Housing Authority so a HUD grant could fund Flock products. Crisp agreed to make the introduction. MaryvillePrivacy’s writeup calls that pattern using the chief as a sales facilitator for additional Flock sales. Crossville should refuse that playbook.

Nashville Banner: Tennessee politicians split further on Flock and LPRs (Aug. 12, 2026)

The Nashville Banner reported August 12 that opposition to license-plate readers is spreading among Tennessee politicians even as Flock still has four lobbyists registered at the statehouse. The piece quotes Mayor Glenn Jacobs telling the Banner the cameras are not “old school license plate readers,” that Tennessee’s LPR statutes date to 2014 with a minor 2021 update, and that “technology has gotten way far ahead.” Senate Majority Leader Jack Johnson (R-Franklin) said he is “very concerned about the advancement of AI” and that when security and liberty are in tension, “we have to fall on the side of liberty.” Democratic gubernatorial nominee Jerri Green wrote that unregulated mass surveillance threatens constitutional privacy and called for non-negotiable guardrails, including a ban on sharing local data with federal immigration authorities. Republican nominee Marsha Blackburn answered a Banner questionnaire “Yes, I do” support LPRs and Flock cameras, then struck a more careful tone standing with Jacobs and Johnson, drawing a line between reading a plate and “24-hour surveillance.”

In the 2nd Congressional District, Democrat Michaela Barnett agrees with Rep. Tim Burchett that the cameras are mass surveillance. TDOT told the Banner no license agreements have been issued to the Tennessee Highway Patrol for LPRs on state right-of-way. Nashville’s citywide LPR plan remains stalled. This is a politics roundup, not a misuse, cancel, or wrongful-stop Case_ID.

Knox County: $800k year-one PO, then “year 1 of 10” without full cost disclosure (July 31, 2026)

Knox News reports it obtained a Knox County Sheriff’s Office purchase order for year one of a Flock Safety deal at $800,000 for a “Flock Safety Bundle and Flock Safety Professional Services.” KCSO later asked procurement to revise the order from a one-year buy to year one of ten. Procurement Director Mandi Benedict told Knox News her office has not been part of discussions about costs or details for years two through ten. The PO request did not include a service contract, only a Flock order form; KCSO used a cooperative / piggyback purchase (Houston-area Region 4 ESC / OMNIA-style contract path) so the equipment buy did not go through a mayor/commission service-contract vote. Cameras were purchased around April 20; Knox News says mayor and commissioners were not told about the planned surveillance hub until July 6. Some cameras were already installed in East Knox County. Future service contracts over $100,000 still need commission/mayor buy-in, so the pause fight continues into the next sheriff’s term. Ask for the full multi-year cost, the written service terms, and a recorded public vote before any renewal, not just a first-year invoice. August 4 reporting on where those cameras actually went: placement outside the grant’s high-crime zip codes.

Knox County: cameras installed outside the high-crime zones KCSO promised (Aug. 4, 2026)

Knox News reported that KCSO’s state-grant plan named six Knoxville-area zip codes with the highest crime rates (37918, 37917, 37914, 37915, 37920, 37921) and said the agency wanted to avoid overlapping Knoxville PD’s camera network. A month before the Advanced Research Center hub was set to launch, cameras were also going up in rural districts such as Corryton and Mascot, far outside those zip codes. Procurement director Hugh Holt told Knox News the office is putting cameras in the targeted zones and throughout the county, and that “We do not own the hardware; we own the data that it produces.” Commissioner Adam Thompson, who represents Corryton and Mascot, has led the pushback after spotting cameras in his district. County engineering stopped new right-of-way installs pending mayor/commission direction. New cameras remain offline through Spangler’s term (Sept. 1); Commission is set to vote Aug. 31 on a potential ban, after the Aug. 24 workshop. KCSO still had not published a data policy or full public location list as of that reporting.

Knox County: Aug. 24 workshop with Flock reps (no public comment)

After the cancelled July workshop, Knox County scheduled a public workshop for Monday, August 24, 2026 at 1:00 p.m. in the main assembly room of the City-County Building (400 Main Street, Knoxville). Knox County, expansion paused; Commission rejects immediate ban (late July 2026)

After Mayor Glenn Jacobs and commissioners challenged a grant-funded plan for roughly 270-300 additional Flock cameras / ARC capacity, Sheriff Tom Spangler said remaining installs were paused while existing LPRs (reporting cites about 84) stay online with ~30-day retention. Jacobs and Spangler agreed new cameras would stay offline through the end of their terms (Sept. 1). On July 28, Knox County Commission continued the debate into August, demanded written policies on privacy, access, audits, and sharing, and rejected an emergency ban. Incoming elected officials after Sept. 1 inherit the fight. A rescheduled workshop with Flock is set for Aug. 24. Separate July 31 reporting documents an $800,000 year-one purchase order later framed as year one of ten: read that update. State Rep. Jason Zachary and Rep. Tim Burchett are pushing state/federal limits; same coverage notes Knoxville PD runs Axon, not Flock.

On August 9, 2026, Jacobs told Peter Doocy on Fox News’s Sunday Briefing that Flock needs a “nationwide pause” until constitutional guardrails catch up. A Fox News writeup of the interview notes he argued Tennessee’s ALPR statutes (2014, with a minor 2021 update) are outdated for newer Flock-style systems, pointed to documented misuse cases, and said tools must still conform to the Constitution. A Young Americans for Liberty clip from his Kibbe on Liberty interview is on his politicians card.

Sullivan County sheriff suspends 10 Flock cameras and asks for a public vote (Aug. 17, 2026)

WVLT reported that Sheriff Jeffrey Cassidy suspended the Sullivan County Sheriff’s Office’s use of Flock license-plate cameras until further notice. The statement went out Monday afternoon, August 17, 2026. Cassidy cited privacy, oversight, and government-surveillance concerns, and said the cameras had become “political rhetoric and public controversy.” He asked the County Commission to consider a referendum on whether the sheriff’s office should continue to use and fund Flock or other automated license-plate readers. That vote has not been scheduled in the reporting.

WVLT says the office runs 10 Flock cameras, all on state highways. Cassidy still called LPRs a valuable law-enforcement tool for investigations, missing people, and crime-related vehicles. The suspend covers sheriff’s-office use. Private businesses and nearby agencies can still run Flock; a county referendum would not turn those cameras off.

This is a catalogued Tennessee pause in the municipal-cancellations index (National card; CAN-2026-100). It is until further notice, not a reported contract termination. White County’s rescind (CAN-2026-113) and Kingston’s non-renewal (CAN-2026-114) are separate rows.
