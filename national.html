Spotlight · Brookfield, Wisconsin · August 6, 2026 · stale Flock homicide alert / high-risk stop

Brookfield PD holds SUV occupants at gunpoint after an outdated Milwaukee Flock alert

On August 6, 2026, around 4:46 p.m., a Flock Safety camera flagged a vehicle on Interstate 94 in Waukesha County as linked to a Milwaukee homicide with a suspect believed to be armed. CBS 58 archived Aug. 11, 2026 reported that Brookfield officers then ran a high-risk stop on westbound I-94 and ordered two people out of the SUV at gunpoint. Investigators quickly determined the occupants were not the people Milwaukee police were looking for.

Milwaukee PD had already cleared the vehicle from the investigation but had left the alert live in Flock. An MPD spokesperson told local stations, including TMJ4 archived Aug. 8, 2026, “This was not a Flock issue. MPD staff inadvertently did not remove a vehicle alert from the system.” Brookfield Police Chief Christopher Garcia defended his officers in a statement covered by WTMJ archived Aug. 9, 2026, saying they stopped the vehicle that was the subject of the alert and that the stop met reasonable-suspicion and related legal standards. The occupants were released after Milwaukee confirmed it no longer wanted the car or the people.

ACLU of Wisconsin analyst Jon McCray Jones, quoted by CBS 58, called the episode a systemic failure: shared Flock data led another jurisdiction to stop an innocent person at gunpoint. No lawsuit is reported in the sources cited here as of August 11, 2026.

Roseville, California · July 31, 2026 · Flock alert accuracy / plate misreads

Roseville PD found Flock misread plates in 71% of stolen or felony alerts

Business Insider archived Aug. 14, 2026’s Nicole Einbinder reported on July 31, 2026 that Roseville police analyzed Flock alerts from 2023 and 2024 and found the system incorrectly read plates in 71% of the 1,427 alerts flagging vehicles as stolen or used in a felony. Records also describe repeated character swaps (for example, 9 as 8), blurry or delayed captures, and missed vehicles. Roseville said none of those incorrect alerts led to a stop or arrest because officers must verify a plate before stopping; California has no statewide verify-before-stop rule like some other states.

Flock told BI its character-read accuracy exceeds 96% in optimal conditions and blamed Roseville’s atypical rear-only camera setup and older hardware placements. Roseville disputed that performance has clearly improved and said it still meets with Flock twice a month. NYU Policing Project’s Max Isaacs called a 71% misread rate on those alerts “beyond the pale.” This site also cites Roseville’s very low EFF hit ratio (about 0.03% of scans matching wanted lists), a different metric from alert-level OCR error rates.

Cities that cancelled, removed, or let Flock expire

Cities and agencies across the country have terminated contracts, refused renewal, let agreements expire, suspended cameras, or rescinded approvals. The SF Standard / Ban Flock Cameras tally cited on this page reports roughly 82 ended contracts across 28 states (Aug. 2021 to May 2026), a larger aggregator figure than this site’s 117-record catalog (JSON).

Public pressure has ended contracts elsewhere, but quitting Flock is not always quitting ALPR. In many cities, residents speaking at council helped drive cancellations. Some of those same cities then hired Axon, or shop other ALPR brands like Motorola Vigilant, Rekor, or Leonardo ELSAG, for plate readers on the same poles. That swaps the company name. It does not end mass plate scanning. Some pitches go further (Leonardo SignalTrace correlates plates with phone and Bluetooth IDs). Spotlight · Staunton, VA · 2025-12-19 · Terminated · ~6 cameras

Staunton, VA, terminated after the CEO’s “coordinated attack” email

After months of resident opposition at council (a dozen speakers on Dec. 11), Flock CEO Garrett Langley emailed police clients that Flock and partner agencies were under a “coordinated attack” from activists who want to “defund the police, weaken public safety, and normalize lawlessness,” and that they were “trying to turn a public records process into a weapon.” Staunton Police Chief Jim Williams disagreed, and the city cancelled.

“Let’s call this what it is: Flock, and the law enforcement agencies we partner with, are under coordinated attack… from the same activist groups who want to defund the police, weaken public safety, and normalize lawlessness… They’re also trying to turn a public records process into a weapon against you and against us.”

Garrett Langley, Dec. 2025 email to police clients

“What we are seeing here is a group of local citizens who are raising concerns that we could be potentially surveilling private citizens, residents and visitors and using the data for nefarious purposes. These citizens have been exercising their rights to receive answers from me, my staff, and city officials, to include our elected leaders. In short, it is democracy in action.”

Staunton Police Chief Jim Williams, reply to Langley

The city said the CEO’s narrative “does not reflect the city’s values.” Contract terminated Jan. 8, 2026; cameras were later taken down. Williams later said the department was not actively exploring other stationary ALPR systems.
