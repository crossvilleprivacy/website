Misuse and wrongful-stop cases outside Tennessee

Roseville, California · July 31, 2026 · Flock alert accuracy / plate misreads

Roseville PD found Flock misread plates in 71% of stolen or felony alerts

Business Insider’s Nicole Einbinder reported on July 31, 2026 that Roseville police analyzed Flock alerts from 2023 and 2024 and found the system incorrectly read plates in 71% of the 1,427 alerts flagging vehicles as stolen or used in a felony. Records also describe repeated character swaps (for example, 9 as 8), blurry or delayed captures, and missed vehicles. Roseville said none of those incorrect alerts led to a stop or arrest because officers must verify a plate before stopping; California has no statewide verify-before-stop rule like some other states.

Flock told BI its character-read accuracy exceeds 96% in optimal conditions and blamed Roseville’s atypical rear-only camera setup and older hardware placements. Roseville disputed that performance has clearly improved and said it still meets with Flock twice a month. NYU Policing Project’s Max Isaacs called a 71% misread rate on those alerts “beyond the pale.” Separately, this site already cites Roseville’s very low EFF hit ratio (about 0.03% of scans matching wanted lists), a different metric from alert-level OCR error rates.

Plymouth, Minnesota · June 2026 · Flock false alert / high-risk stop

Joel Feder, tracked for days, then boxed in over a plate that was never stolen

Automotive journalist Joel Feder (The Drive) and his wife were leaving a Kohl’s parking lot in Plymouth when four squad cars boxed them in. Officers ordered hands up; Feder was patted down. Body-cam and Feder’s own account show police had been following Flock plate alerts on the Range Rover press loaner for days, then staged the stop when a camera pinged the Kohl’s lot.

The alert chain started with a bad database entry, not a stolen car. A New Jersey manufacturer plate reported lost in California was logged incomplete as 34 DTM (missing the small middle digits). Feder’s actual plate was 34 10 DTM. According to Feder’s account and later police reporting, the Flock alert did not distinguish those small middle characters, and the network treated the loaner as a hit and kept notifying Plymouth PD. The VIN came back clean; the police report later confirmed the original plate had been lost, not stolen, and was entered wrong.

Sources: The Drive: Feder first-person account · FOX 9 Minneapolis · CBS Minnesota / WCCO · Catalogued as ID 42 in the AI wrongful-enforcement database and FLK-2026-015 in the misuse master database.

Sumter County, Florida · July 2026 · alleged personal-use searches / agency suspension

Detective arrested and terminated; sheriff suspends Flock pending a complete audit

On July 23, the Sumter County Sheriff’s Office announced that an internal audit had flagged Detective Brandy Almany’s use of restricted law-enforcement databases. The agency alleges that Almany used the Flock ALPR system, Florida’s Driver and Vehicle Information Database (DAVID), and the Comprehensive Case Information System (CCIS) to obtain information about her husband’s ex-wife for personal reasons. Investigators further allege that she used unrelated case numbers and created false electronic records to make the searches appear connected to legitimate investigations.

Almany was arrested on charges of official misconduct and offenses against computer users and was terminated from the agency. Those are allegations, not a finding of guilt. Sheriff Patrick Breeden said he ordered a complete internal audit of the databases and suspended the agency’s use of Flock while the review proceeds. The agency had not announced when or whether the cameras would return as of July 24.

Charlotte, North Carolina · July 29 to Aug. 4, 2026 · alleged Flock misuse / arrest · MOU released

CMPD officer charged after allegedly using Flock to ID an undercover vehicle for a friend

On July 29, 2026, Charlotte-Mecklenburg Police Officer Seth Daniel Elliott was arrested and charged with illegally accessing a government computer. An SBI affidavit summarized in local reporting says a friend who was the target of a Watauga County drug investigation contacted Elliott on June 16 and asked him to run a plate. Investigators allege Elliott queried the plate in Flock Safety and CJLEADS, learned it belonged to an undercover officer’s vehicle, and relayed that information back over Snapchat. CMPD placed Elliott on unpaid administrative leave and revoked database access while the case proceeds. Allegations and charging documents as reported, not a finding of guilt by this site.

On August 4, after pressure from WBTV, CMPD released its Flock Safety memorandum of understanding. The department says it does not own Flock cameras; officers get network access through the MOU, including “limited access” to community devices (businesses, HOAs, schools). Searches are supposed to be for “bona fide investigations and evidence gathering.” The MOU was signed Feb. 18 by Chief Estella Patterson; the original trial period was set to end in May, but a spokesperson said it was extended. Crossville lesson: even without owning poles, an agency MOU can put officers onto a statewide / national search network.

Bexar County, Texas · July 29, 2026 · expansion amid privacy concerns

Bexar County Flock network keeps growing as advocates warn about warrantless mass capture

Texas Public Radio reported on July 29, 2026 that automated license-plate readers are spreading across Bexar County. The Bexar County Sheriff’s Office alone operates 33 Flock cameras; its public dashboard recently showed more than 750,000 vehicle detections in a 30-day window. Area cities and HOAs also run Flock. Local departments credit stolen-vehicle arrests; ACLU of Texas and other advocates argue agencies are building searchable mass-surveillance infrastructure without warrants, probable cause, or narrowly tailored search rules.

Bexar County says searches must connect to criminal investigations and that vehicle data is deleted after 30 days. Flock says customers control their data and the company does not sell it. Suggested reforms in the piece include warrants for database searches, short retention, marked camera locations, and penalties for personal misuse, the same oversight gap Crossville faces before any renewal.
