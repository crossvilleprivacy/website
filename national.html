Nationwide pattern

Misuse and wrongful-stop cases outside Tennessee

These cases are not Crossville’s. They show how the same kind of plate-reader stack fails elsewhere, false alerts, stalking, and high-risk stops. Crossville still needs a published search policy, partner list, and monthly use reports before any renewal.

Reading this section: Where someone was charged, pleaded, or sentenced, we say so and link sources. Where reporting describes an accusation or investigation without a final public disposition, we label it as an allegation or as attributed reporting, not as a finding of guilt by this site.

Plymouth, Minnesota · June 2026 · Flock false alert / high-risk stop

Joel Feder, tracked for days, then boxed in over a plate that was never stolen

Automotive journalist Joel Feder (The Drive) and his wife were leaving a Kohl’s parking lot in Plymouth when four squad cars boxed them in. Officers ordered hands up; Feder was patted down. Body-cam and Feder’s own account show police had been following Flock plate alerts on the Range Rover press loaner for days, then staged the stop when a camera pinged the Kohl’s lot.

The alert chain started with a bad database entry, not a stolen car. A New Jersey manufacturer plate reported lost in California was logged incomplete as 34 DTM (missing the small middle digits). Feder’s actual plate was 34 10 DTM. According to Feder’s account and later police reporting, the Flock alert did not distinguish those small middle characters, and the network treated the loaner as a hit and kept notifying Plymouth PD. The VIN came back clean; the police report later confirmed the original plate had been lost, not stolen, and was entered wrong.

Sources: The Drive: Feder first-person account · FOX 9 Minneapolis · CBS Minnesota / WCCO · Catalogued as ID 42 in the AI wrongful-enforcement database and FLK-2026-015 in the misuse master database.

Sumter County, Florida · July 2026 · alleged personal-use searches / agency suspension

Detective arrested and terminated; sheriff suspends Flock pending a complete audit

On July 23, the Sumter County Sheriff’s Office announced that an internal audit had flagged Detective Brandy Almany’s use of restricted law-enforcement databases. The agency alleges that Almany used the Flock ALPR system, Florida’s Driver and Vehicle Information Database (DAVID), and the Comprehensive Case Information System (CCIS) to obtain information about her husband’s ex-wife for personal reasons. Investigators further allege that she used unrelated case numbers and created false electronic records to make the searches appear connected to legitimate investigations.

Almany was arrested on charges of official misconduct and offenses against computer users and was terminated from the agency. Those are allegations, not a finding of guilt. Sheriff Patrick Breeden said he ordered a complete internal audit of the databases and suspended the agency’s use of Flock while the review proceeds. The agency had not announced when or whether the cameras would return as of July 24.

Sources: WESH 2, sheriff statement and charges · WFTV 9, arrest, termination, and Flock suspension · News 6 / ClickOrlando, internal-audit details · FLK-2026-020 in the misuse database

Baytown, Texas · July to August 2026 · alleged Flock misuse / resignation amid criminal probe

Baytown PD investigating officer accused of misusing Flock; officer later resigns

In late July 2026, Baytown Police Chief John Stringer announced that the department had received allegations that one of its officers misused the city’s Flock Safety license plate reader system. Stringer authorized both an internal affairs investigation (for possible policy violations) and a separate criminal investigation, and asked the Harris County District Attorney’s Office to assist on the criminal side for objectivity and transparency.

Chron reported on August 2, 2026 that the officer resigned amid the open investigation. The department has not publicly identified the officer or detailed the specific allegations. Those remain allegations under investigation, not a finding of guilt by a court or by this site. Chron framed Baytown alongside Pasadena and Fort Bend County as a Houston-area cluster of Flock-misuse probes.

Sources: Click2Houston / KPRC 2, chief statement and DA referral · ABC13, investigation announcement · Houston Chronicle (July) · Chron: Houston-area cluster / resignation (Aug. 2, 2026) · KPRC 2 report (YouTube) · FLK-2026-018 in the misuse database

Fort Bend County, Texas · 2022–2025 searches · discipline reported July 2026

Sheriff’s lieutenant admitted 148 personal Flock plate searches; two-day suspension

Internal-affairs documents obtained by ABC13 and summarized by Chron show a Fort Bend County Sheriff’s Office lieutenant searched one woman’s license plate about 148 times (and another plate about 41 times) from 2022 through 2025. The lieutenant admitted the searches were not for official sheriff’s-office purposes. The woman reported fearing “stalking” after checking Have I Been Flocked. Reporting says the agency found a general-orders violation and imposed a two-day suspension. Allegations and agency discipline as reported, not a criminal conviction catalogued here.

Chron’s August 2 Houston-area roundup places Fort Bend next to Pasadena and Baytown as evidence that personal Flock lookups keep surfacing even where departments say the tools “only track vehicles.”

Sources: ABC13: Fort Bend lieutenant / IA docs · Chron: 3 Houston-area agencies (Aug. 2, 2026)

Charlotte, North Carolina · July 29–30, 2026 · alleged Flock misuse / arrest

CMPD officer charged after allegedly using Flock to ID an undercover vehicle for a friend

On July 29, 2026, Charlotte-Mecklenburg Police Officer Seth Daniel Elliott was arrested and charged with illegally accessing a government computer. An SBI affidavit summarized in local reporting says a friend who was the target of a Watauga County drug investigation contacted Elliott on June 16 and asked him to run a plate. Investigators allege Elliott queried the plate in Flock Safety and CJLEADS, learned it belonged to an undercover officer’s vehicle, and relayed that information back over Snapchat. CMPD placed Elliott on unpaid administrative leave and revoked database access while the case proceeds. Allegations and charging documents as reported, not a finding of guilt by this site.

Sources: WBTV: officer accused of tipping a drug target (July 30, 2026) · Spectrum News: CMPD officer charged · WSOC: warrants detail the Flock search

Georgia · June to July 2026 · alleged Flock misuse / firings & arrests

Georgia agencies fire and charge officers in a fast-moving Flock misuse wave

Local and statewide reporting in June and July 2026 describe a cluster of Georgia law-enforcement employees fired and/or arrested after audits allegedly found personal or non-law-enforcement Flock license-plate searches. Coverage includes Cherokee County deputies, five former Albany Police Department officers, Fayetteville officers flagged during audit-tool testing, a Henry County officer, a DeKalb County Sheriff’s sergeant, and a Conyers Real-Time Crime Center supervisor, among others. Atlanta News First reported identifying at least 20 Georgia cases in the past year with discipline and/or criminal allegations tied to improper Flock use. Late July added Savannah PD’s six-employee leave / GBI referral.

Those are agency allegations and pending cases, not findings of guilt by this site. Departments and Flock have pointed to new audit-assistance tooling; critics say the volume of cases shows how easy personal lookups remain without warrant rules.

Savannah, Georgia · July 31, 2026 · admin leave / GBI referral

Six Savannah PD employees placed on leave over alleged Flock misuse

Savannah Police announced that an internal audit under Flock’s Audit Assistance Program flagged potential misuse by four officers and two civilian employees. The department placed all six on administrative leave, said discipline up to termination is on the table, and referred the cases to the Georgia Bureau of Investigation for an independent criminal probe. SPD says the alleged searches were unrelated to law-enforcement purposes (including personal acquaintances and family members), and that one officer allegedly shared SPD Flock access with personnel from an outside agency. Internal investigation began July 27; matters remain allegations pending investigation, not findings of guilt by this site.

Sources: WJCL: six employees on leave (July 31, 2026) · WTOC · WRDW (Aug. 2, 2026) · Georgia July wave

Detective who investigated Flock stalking is himself charged over Flock and GPS tracking

Milwaukee Police Department internal affairs had investigated Officer Josue Ayala for alleged personal Flock searches of a romantic partner and that partner’s ex (about 179 lookups in earlier reporting). In July 2026, local and national coverage reported that Detective Tehrangi Chapman, one of the investigators on that case, was himself charged after allegedly using Flock to track people in his own life and secretly installing a GPS device on one person’s vehicle. Those are charging allegations, not a conviction.

Greer Police terminated Kareem Lynch (June 26) and Sebastian Echeverry (June 29) after an internal investigation alleged unauthorized Flock searches. National · July 2026 · Mother Jones / Institute for Justice stalking roundup

Mother Jones: police keep losing jobs for using Flock to stalk people

A July 2026 Mother Jones report summarized the romantic-partner stalking pattern across Flock / ALPR systems: Milwaukee’s Ayala case, then IA Det. Tehrangi Chapman charged while investigating that same case, plus firings and charges in Illinois, South Carolina, Texas, California, and Georgia. The Institute for Justice catalog cited in the piece identified at least 24 similar ALPR stalking cases in two years, nearly all ending in criminal charges and job loss. Mother Jones also quotes ACLU counsel on how vehicle travel histories reveal private life, and notes Flock’s response pointing to its audit-assistance tool.

Those case counts come from reporting and IJ’s catalog, individual matters remain allegations or pending cases unless a court has ruled. Crossville already tracks several of the named local stories ( Chapman, Georgia, Greer, Pasadena, Baytown).

Bexar County, Texas · July 29, 2026 · expansion amid privacy concerns

Bexar County Flock network keeps growing as advocates warn about warrantless mass capture

Texas Public Radio reported on July 29, 2026 that automated license-plate readers are spreading across Bexar County. The Bexar County Sheriff’s Office alone operates 33 Flock cameras; its public dashboard recently showed more than 750,000 vehicle detections in a 30-day window. Area cities and HOAs also run Flock. Local departments credit stolen-vehicle arrests; ACLU of Texas and other advocates argue agencies are building searchable mass-surveillance infrastructure without warrants, probable cause, or narrowly tailored search rules.

Bexar County says searches must connect to criminal investigations and that vehicle data is deleted after 30 days. Flock says customers control their data and the company does not sell it. Suggested reforms in the piece include warrants for database searches, short retention, marked camera locations, and penalties for personal misuse, the same oversight gap Crossville faces before any renewal.

Sources: TPR: Flock expands across Bexar County

Texas · August 3, 2026 · Texas Tribune statewide surge report

Texas Tribune: Flock cameras surge statewide as DPS expands partner sharing

An August 3, 2026 Texas Tribune / Waco Bridge investigation described Flock’s rapid expansion across Texas even as cities cancel or protest. Advocates estimate more than 10,000 cameras in the state. DPS declined to disclose its own camera count but confirmed data-sharing partnerships with 206 local agencies (up from 122 in February). Reporting also cites a roughly $26 million DPS Flock contract tied to state roadway ALPRs and Operation Lone Star, plus local subsidy patterns (including Motor Vehicle Crime Prevention Authority grants).

The piece documents Baytown’s open misuse probe, officer resignation, Austin and Bandera cancellations, Dallas reporting 25 camera attacks since March, Houston vandalism investigations, and Rep. Keith Self’s PRIVACY Act requiring federal warrants before querying state/local ALPR data. Crossville lesson: statewide sharing networks can grow quietly even when individual cities exit.

Sources: Texas Tribune: Use of Flock cameras surges in Texas (Aug. 3, 2026)

Municipal cancellations

Cities that cancelled, removed, or let Flock expire

Cities and agencies across the country have terminated contracts, refused renewal, let agreements expire, suspended cameras, or rescinded approvals. The SF Standard / Ban Flock Cameras tally cited in this site’s cancellations dossier summary reports roughly 82 ended contracts across 28 states (Aug. 2021 to May 2026), a larger aggregator figure than this site’s 49-record verified dossier (JSON).

Public pressure has ended contracts elsewhere, but quitting Flock is not always quitting ALPR. In many cities, residents speaking at council helped drive cancellations. Some of those same cities then hired Axon, or shop other ALPR brands like Motorola Vigilant, Rekor, or Leonardo ELSAG, for plate readers on the same poles. That swaps the company name. It does not end mass plate scanning. Some pitches go further (Leonardo SignalTrace correlates plates with phone and Bluetooth IDs). Read Not just Flock, other vendors, and Gadget Review: Cities Are Ditching Flock Safety Cameras - Then Hiring Axon to Do the Same Job (July 20, 2026).

Full dossier: PDF · Excel · JSON · More coverage: NPR · State of Surveillance.

Spotlight, company trust and resident pressure

Examples that matter for Crossville: cities that ended Flock after trust failures (including a vendor turning cameras back on without telling the town); cities that removed cameras and paused before buying a replacement; and cities that quit Flock only to hire Axon.

Spotlight · Narragansett, RI · August 3, 2026 · Terminated · ~4 cameras

Narragansett, RI, unanimous vote to end Flock early

On August 3, 2026, Narragansett Town Council voted unanimously to terminate the town’s Flock contract early and accept early-exit penalties. The agreement had auto-renewed for two years after a trial and was set to run into September 2027. Coverage notes roughly four cameras in operation and cites low local crime as a reason some councilors said the system was unnecessary. The vote came one week after South Kingstown’s 5-0 cancel, continuing Rhode Island’s late-July / early-August exit wave.

Sources: WPRI: Narragansett to stop using ALPRs · WJAR / TurnTo10 (Aug. 4, 2026) · South Kingstown cancel

Spotlight · Littleton, MA · July 30 to Aug. 2, 2026 · Terminated · unauthorized reactivation

Littleton cancels after Flock reactivated powered-down cameras without notice

In May 2026, Littleton’s Select Board ordered the town’s six Flock cameras temporarily powered down so officials could review the technology and privacy policy. On July 30, when Select Board Chair Gary Wilson asked police to check the system’s status, officers found that five of the six cameras on public ways had been reactivated by Flock Safety with no notification to the town.

The Select Board immediately directed Littleton Electric Light & Water to cut power to each unit, instructed Town Counsel to terminate the Flock contract, and ordered Public Works to remove all six cameras from public ways. Local reporting does not say how long the cameras had been back on before discovery. Crossville lesson: a city that “pauses” Flock may still not control whether the vendor’s hardware stays dark unless power is cut and the contract is ended.

Sources: Shrewsbury Post: cancel after unauthorized reactivation (Aug. 2, 2026) · Lowell Sun (July 31, 2026)

Spotlight · El Cerrito, CA · July 31, 2026 · physical removal after non-renewal

El Cerrito begins taking down Flock cameras after federal-access non-renewal

KALW reported on July 31, 2026 that El Cerrito police had begun physically removing the city’s Flock cameras. In May, council voted not to renew after federal agencies accessed data from about 40 cameras without local police knowledge. The contract expired June 6 and cameras were supposed to be offline, but residents raised alarms when video showed a unit still flickering as cars passed. Flock told the city the removal process would run through August 18. Bay Area context already on this page includes Mountain View, Los Altos Hills, and Santa Cruz exits.

Sources: KALW: El Cerrito begins removing Flock cameras (July 31, 2026) · CC Pulse: May non-renewal vote

Spotlight · Harrisonburg, VA · 2026-07-29 · Terminated · ~31 cameras

Harrisonburg, VA, cancelled with a fixed-ALPR approval policy

On July 29, 2026, Harrisonburg City Council voted 4-0 to end the city’s Flock contract and adopt a policy that generally prohibits mounted automatic license plate readers without express council approval (with limited exceptions). City Manager Ande Banks said the system would go dark by Friday (July 31) and that staff had already submitted a work order for camera removal, with the city ready to remove and store the hardware if Flock lagged. Reporting puts about 31 Falcon cameras (plus Flex units) on the contract since mid-2023.

Local organizers (Deflock Harrisonburg) celebrated the cancel while noting council did not yet pass a broader ban on all future mass-surveillance vendors. Regional context in the same coverage: Elkton ended Flock the prior week; Staunton and Charlottesville had already quit earlier.

Sources: WHSV / 29News, council vote · Daily News-Record, policy + cancel · The Center Square

Spotlight · Salem, MA · 2026-07 · Non-renewal · vendor-swap risk

Salem, MA, ending Flock over data ownership; seeking another vendor

In late July 2026, Salem Mayor Dominick Pangallo and Police Chief Lucas Miller said the city would not renew Flock and would remove installed ALPRs, citing “insufficient protections” for ALPR data and arguing that data should be owned and used only for Salem’s public-safety priorities. They also said Flock units would be replaced with systems that “better retain local control of data”, a classic cancel-then-replace pattern Crossville should watch (see Not just Flock).

Massachusetts context already on this page: Framingham non-renewal (June 30, 2026), Cambridge’s unauthorized-install termination, and earlier Natick / Watertown exits.

Sources: Boston.com: Salem joins MA reject list · WCVB: Salem ending Flock · CAN-2026-046 in the cancellations database

Spotlight · Monroe County, IN · 2026-07-28 · Terminated · ~6 cameras

Monroe County, IN, ditches Flock over privacy / outside-access risk

Monroe County Commissioners voted to cancel the county’s Flock contract (~6 cameras), calling the system mass surveillance and citing risk that plate and location data could be accessed by outside or federal agencies. Coverage notes Bloomington is also ending its Flock deal; Commissioner Jody Madeira stressed that cameras must come down, not only contracts ending.

Sources: CBS4 Indy: Monroe County ditches Flock · WIBC · CBS4 Indy (YouTube) · CAN-2026-047 in the cancellations database

Spotlight · Stoughton, WI · 2026-07-28 · Terminated · 5 cameras

Stoughton, WI, unanimous vote to terminate Flock

After months of packed meetings and resident opposition, Stoughton City Council unanimously terminated Flock on July 28, 2026, about three months after install. The city will pay a ~$12,500 early-exit buyout on a two-year / $25,000 contract, remove five cameras (four ALPR + one live feed), and delete collected data. Council also committed not to swap in similar continuous monitoring without at least 30 days’ notice and public input. Local reporting calls Stoughton the seventh Dane County entity ending Flock.

Sources: WMTV 15, unanimous terminate vote · Channel 3000 · WKOW 27 (YouTube) · CAN-2026-048 in the cancellations database

Spotlight · South Kingstown, RI · 2026-07-27 · Terminated · ~6-7 cameras

South Kingstown, RI, unanimous cancel after ACLU letter and resident push

South Kingstown Town Council voted 5-0 on July 27, 2026 to terminate Flock and remove cameras as soon as possible after hundreds of resident emails and an ACLU of Rhode Island advocacy letter. The ~$30,300 year contract had gone active when cameras went up in February (~6-7 units). The town is seeking a partial refund; Rhode Island State Police Flock on state roads may still operate nearby. One week later, Narragansett voted unanimously to exit as well.

Sources: WPRI: South Kingstown to stop using ALPRs · WJAR / TurnTo10, ends partnership · ACLU of Rhode Island · WPRI (YouTube) · WJAR (YouTube) · CAN-2026-049 in the cancellations database

Spotlight · Los Angeles PD · 2026-07-11 expire · renegotiating

LAPD, let Flock expire over civil-liberties concerns, then started renegotiating

In mid-July 2026, the Los Angeles Police Department allowed its multi-year Flock agreement to expire. LAPD Chief Information Officer Dean Gialamas cited “serious concerns around civil liberties and civil rights,” especially privacy and collected data. An Inspector General audit earlier that month had urged pausing new deployments until enforceable privacy, security, and oversight rules were in place; reporting put about 138 pole-mounted Flock cameras on the city program.

Within days, Police Commission coverage said LAPD was renegotiating for stronger contractual privacy and data-ownership language, so this is a pause / rewrite fight, not necessarily a permanent exit. Separately, LAPD’s Inspector General work on false stolen-car alerts (about 1 in 3 wrong in the sampled window) is covered in the vendor dossier because Axon and Motorola were also named in that multi-vendor program.

Sources: ABC7: LAPD ending agreement · Los Angeles Times, suspends use · Fortune, renegotiating · TechCrunch · Business Insider

Spotlight · Huntington, WV · July 2026 · ACLU lawsuit after $2.1M approval

Huntington, WV: ACLU sues after contested Flock contract vote

Huntington City Council approved a roughly $2.1 million Flock contract on a 6-4 vote after a marathon meeting with dozens of public speakers, nearly all opposed. The ACLU of West Virginia (with co-counsel) then sued in Cabell County Circuit Court on behalf of resident Greg Jimison, alleging the city skipped required procedures for letting a private company use city property for commercial purposes, and arguing the approval should be treated as void. ACLU-WV said further challenges to the contract itself could follow.

Sources: WV MetroNews: ACLU suit after $2.1M approval · West Virginia Public Broadcasting: ACLU suit

Spotlight · Wichita, KS · July 30–31, 2026 · Constitutional lawsuit

Wichita resident sues over warrantless Flock ALPR dragnet

Machinist and privacy advocate Mason Grimmett, represented by the Kansas Justice Institute, sued the City of Wichita in Sedgwick County District Court seeking a declaratory judgment that the city’s Flock Safety ALPR program violates the Kansas Constitution. The suit asks the court to end the program and purge historical plate/location records held by Wichita and other agencies that store Wichita data. Reporting describes nearly 200 cameras and roughly 900,000 vehicle photos a month. The complaint and KJI materials highlight searchable travel history and association tools (including convoy-style analysis), the same class of features Crossville should demand written limits on. This is a filed lawsuit seeking prospective relief, not a final court ruling.

Sources: Kansas Reflector (July 31, 2026) · KCUR (Aug. 1, 2026) · Wichita Eagle · Kansas Justice Institute case page · Petition PDF · Multi-Geo & Convoy

Spotlight · Lima, OH · August 1, 2026 · Local scrutiny

Lima News covers rising Flock surveillance concerns

The Lima News reported on August 1, 2026 that Flock cameras face growing local and national scrutiny. Lima PD authorizes 12 cameras at major entry and exit points. The piece notes documented officer misuse of ALPR networks elsewhere, resident worry about retention and third-party sharing, and Flock’s claim that agencies control their own data and typical retention is about 30 days unless an agency changes settings. Useful as a mid-size-city example of the same trust gap Crossville has when no city-published search policy or monthly use reports are online.

Sources: Lima News: Flock cameras raise surveillance concerns (Aug. 1, 2026)

Spotlight · Redwood City, CA · August 1, 2026 · competitive RFP after opposition

Redwood City seeks new ALPR proposals instead of auto-renewing Flock

Redwood City opened a competitive request for proposals for plate-reader equipment and support after residents urged the city to end its Flock relationship. The current deal would have auto-renewed; the city told Flock it must bid again. The RFP covers about 25 readers, a three-year purchase with optional two-year extension, and a proposal deadline of Aug. 7 (award expected Sept. 14). Coverage notes Bay Area cities that already quit Flock (Mountain View, Santa Cruz, Los Altos Hills) and says Redwood City is demanding documented contractual safeguards on who can search the network. This is not a cancel vote yet, it is a forced rebid under public pressure.

Sources: Redwood City Pulse (Aug. 1, 2026)

Spotlight · Columbus, OH · Aug. 10, 2026 hearing · statewide sharing paused

Columbus sets public hearing after audit flags thousands of out-of-policy Flock searches

Axios Columbus reports Mayor Andrew Ginther ordered Columbus police to stop statewide Flock data sharing after an audit found Columbus’s network was searched about 15,000 times for immigration-related queries. Council Member Emmanuel Remy, who requested the audit, set a special Public Safety & Criminal Justice Committee hearing for Aug. 10 to examine the findings; Flock representatives were invited. Remy’s release framed the hearing as transparency and accountability: whether safeguards were enough and what changes are needed. Crossville lesson: without published partner lists, monthly search reports, and written sharing rules, residents cannot see who is querying local plates.

Sources: Axios Columbus: public hearing on Flock cameras

Spotlight · Gwinnett County, GA · 2026-07-29 · public cancel demand · still under contract

Gwinnett County, GA, residents pack the mic to demand a Flock cancel

On July 29, 2026, Gwinnett County commissioners heard a wave of speakers calling for cancellation of the county’s large Flock deployment (local reporting cites on the order of 1,100 license-plate readers and a multi-year deal expanded past about $2.2 million, running into 2029). Speakers raised privacy and federal-access fears; county police defended investigative value. This is organized opposition under an active contract, not a cancel vote yet, and sits next to Georgia’s broader 2026 Flock-misuse wave documented above.

Sources: WSB-TV: Gwinnett cancel demand · Georgia July misuse wave

Spotlight · U.S. House · July 15, 2026 · H.R. 9716 PRIVACY Act

Rep. Keith Self, PRIVACY Act would require warrants for federal ALPR access

On July 15, 2026, Texas Rep. Keith Self introduced H.R. 9716, the Protecting Rights in Video and Equipment Acquired Discovery (PRIVACY) Act, with Reps. Eli Crane and Andrew Clyde as original cosponsors. The bill would generally bar federal law enforcement from accessing or querying covered state and local surveillance data, including automated license-plate systems, without a federal warrant. It also pushes a short default retention window for warrant-obtained data, directs creation of a “Jurisdictional Wall List” of covered technologies, and limits federal funding to buy or install those systems. The bill does not ban local Flock use; it targets federal backdoor queries of local camera networks.

Crossville already tracks Tennessee Rep. Tim Burchett’s related H.R. 9800 and Kentucky Rep. Thomas Massie’s federal-funding tease. Self’s bill is the introduced warrant-focused companion in that federal packet.

Sources: GovInfo: H.R. 9716 (introduced text) · Daily Caller News Foundation: Self PRIVACY Act (July 15, 2026) · Texas Scorecard · Texas Tribune statewide surge

Spotlight · U.S. House · 2026-07-27 · proposed federal funding restriction

Rep. Thomas Massie, bill tease to withhold federal money from Flock-style camera cities

On July 27, 2026, Kentucky Rep. Thomas Massie said he would soon sponsor legislation to withhold federal funds from municipalities and police departments that deploy Flock and similar mass-surveillance cameras against “law abiding citizens.” Formal bill text was not yet released in that coverage. Crossville already tracks Tennessee Rep. Tim Burchett’s related federal H.R. 9800 (Protection Against Mass Surveillance Act), which names Flock cameras in the introduced bill text, and Texas Rep. Keith Self’s H.R. 9716 PRIVACY Act warrant requirement for federal ALPR access.

Sources: Newsweek: Massie funding bill · WUKY: Massie tease · Burchett H.R. 9800

Spotlight · Staunton, VA · 2025-12-19 · Terminated · ~6 cameras

Staunton, VA, terminated after the CEO’s “coordinated attack” email

After months of resident opposition at council (a dozen speakers on Dec. 11), Flock CEO Garrett Langley emailed police clients that Flock and partner agencies were under a “coordinated attack” from activists who want to “defund the police, weaken public safety, and normalize lawlessness,” and that they were “trying to turn a public records process into a weapon.” Staunton Police Chief Jim Williams disagreed, and the city cancelled.

“Let’s call this what it is: Flock, and the law enforcement agencies we partner with, are under coordinated attack… from the same activist groups who want to defund the police, weaken public safety, and normalize lawlessness… They’re also trying to turn a public records process into a weapon against you and against us.”

Garrett Langley, Dec. 2025 email to police clients

“What we are seeing here is a group of local citizens who are raising concerns that we could be potentially surveilling private citizens, residents and visitors and using the data for nefarious purposes. These citizens have been exercising their rights to receive answers from me, my staff, and city officials, to include our elected leaders. In short, it is democracy in action.”

Staunton Police Chief Jim Williams, reply to Langley

The city said the CEO’s narrative “does not reflect the city’s values.” Contract terminated Jan. 8, 2026; cameras were later taken down. Williams later said the department was not actively exploring other stationary ALPR systems.

Sources: Augusta Free Press, cancel / CEO ideology · City of Staunton, terminates Flock contract · WHSV, contract terminated · Augusta Free Press, cameras taken down · Flock dossier: Langley email · CAN-2025-003 in the cancellations database.

Spotlight · Fort Collins, CO · 2026-06-16 vote · cameras removed by 2026-07-07

Fort Collins, CO, cancelled and physically removed

City Council voted 6-1 on June 16, 2026 to immediately cancel Flock, stop data collection, remove cameras, and delay seeking new ALPR bids until a citywide surveillance policy is in place. Staff confirmed all 15 city Flock cameras were removed by early July. Private Flock cameras elsewhere in the area were not covered by the city vote.

Sources: Coloradoan, council cancel vote · Coloradoan, cameras removed · Denver7 · CAN-2026-043 in the cancellations database

Spotlight · Syracuse, NY · Flock removed mid-July 2026 · Axon replacement

Syracuse, NY: Flock off city property; Axon next

After data-sharing controversies, Common Council moved to end Flock on city streets and approved a multi-year Axon plate-reader contract. By mid-July 2026, Flock confirmed its 13 city-approved readers were removed from city property. Axon cameras were expected to go up afterward, a vendor swap, not an end to mass plate scanning. Separate Flock installs (e.g. university / private) can remain.

Sources: Spectrum News 1, city Flock removed · Central Current, removal / private cameras remain · Not just Flock / Axon

Spotlight · Oshkosh, WI · 2026-04-23 · Rescinded · ~26 cameras

Oshkosh, WI, rescinded

Council approved a one-year renewal amid vocal public opposition, then unanimously rescinded within ~24 hours after Police Chief Dean Smith said, per local reporting, that Flock misrepresented heat-map tracking capability at the council meeting. Cameras powered off and bagged.

Sources: Post-Crescent · WBAY · CAN-2026-001 in the cancellations database.

Spotlight · Verona, WI · 2025-12 · Non-renewal

Verona, WI, non-renewal

Common Council voted not to renew; contract lapsed December. Officials and residents framed the decision as lack of public trust in Flock Safety. Cameras remained up for months; city covered them pending physical removal.

Sources: Wisconsin Examiner · CAN-2026-002 in the cancellations database.
