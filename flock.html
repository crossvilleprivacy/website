Who is Flock Safety?

Flock Safety is a private, venture-backed company that sells a subscription surveillance service to police, cities, businesses, and HOAs, not a one-time camera purchase. Typical Flock contracts keep ownership of the hardware with Flock; the city pays recurring fees for installed cameras plus cloud search, alerts, and network access. Public debate is not “anti-law enforcement”, it is residents asking how a private vendor’s database is governed, audited, shared, retained, and paid for with tax dollars.

How the product actually works

Cameras capture an image of every passing vehicle plus time and location metadata. Data uploads to a private cloud dashboard. Agencies subscribe for search, alert, and analytics tools. A public Flock services agreement archived June 10, 2025 states that the Hardware remains Flock’s personal property, the agency must help Flock keep title, and Flock will remove the devices when the subscription ends. The company makes money from storing, searching, and sharing the data, not from selling the city a camera it owns.

Beyond plate lookups, Flock markets FreeForm (natural-language AI search across LPR and video) and tools like Multi-Geo and Convoy Search that track cross-city travel and vehicles that move together. That is a searchable database of how people and cars look and where they go, not a simple fixed camera.

Plain-language definition when people say “Flock sells your data”: Flock sells access to a platform built from public-space captures, plus optional analytics. Even if the vendor frames outputs as “not personal data,” it is still a paid movement database paid for by taxpayers.

Why Crossville residents should care

  • Crossville PD runs Flock Falcons per Quote Q-00234 archived Aug. 18, 2026 (10 Falcons archived Aug. 18, 2026).
  • Scans of ordinary drivers can enter the same shared vendor network used elsewhere in Tennessee, including departments with documented personal-use misuse (Oak Ridge).
  • Flock's template contract grants the company a "perpetual, irrevocable, worldwide, royalty-free" license to use agency data as "Aggregated Data" for marketing, product development, and "crime prevention efforts." Even if a department clicks "don't share" in Flock's settings, the contract language may override that choice for "investigative purposes." The ACLU archived Aug. 25, 2026 found this clause in contracts across 80+ Massachusetts departments; Boston PD is the only major department known to have negotiated it out. Public contracts from El Cerrito, CA archived Sept. 3, 2026 and Pascagoula, MS archived Feb. 2, 2023 contain the same language. Crossville should publish its live agreement so residents can read the local terms. See also: Why this matters: the fine print.
  • Independent reporting has also documented Flock using overseas gig workers to annotate U.S. camera imagery for AI training, see overseas AI annotation.

The city does not buy the cameras. It rents a vendor cloud.

Crossville is not purchasing pole cameras it will own. Typical Flock contracts keep the hardware as Flock’s property. The city pays a subscription for installed devices plus cloud search, alerts, and network access. Flock’s own FAQ archived Aug. 18, 2026 includes questions titled “Why can’t I buy Flock Safety cameras?” and “What happens after my subscription ends?” The public Whitestown, IN Flock services agreement archived June 10, 2025 states, in the security-interest clause: “The Hardware shall remain the personal property of Flock and will be removed upon the termination or expiration of this Agreement.” Agency there also agrees to acts needed “to assure the retention of title of the Hardware by Flock.” Crossville has not published its live agreement, so residents should not assume every clause matches Whitestown.

Those leased devices are one node in a software platform. Flock’s Product Hub archived July 27, 2026 lists FlockOS, FreeForm, a national LPR network, video cameras, gunshot and sound detection, and drones on one catalog page, not a stand-alone camera:

  • Search and alerts in a vendor dashboard. The same FAQ lists footage search, Hot List alerts, and a Safe List. Flock’s Safe List explainer archived Nov. 3, 2025 says registered neighborhood plates can be marked as resident vehicles and filtered out of a search.
  • Software on other cameras. Flock’s FAQ includes “Can I use Flock Safety software on my traditional IP cameras?” and “What does the video integration add to traditional IP cameras?” Those are Flock’s own question titles. Crossville has not published whether any such integration is enabled locally.
  • APIs that move data automatically. Flock’s Developer Hub archived Aug. 22, 2026 describes “Flock Apps” that query vehicle detections, subscribe to real-time events, register outside sensors as devices, and run vehicle images from other cameras through Flock’s AI (Vehicle Detection API). That same overview says endpoints like vehicle-detection search require a reason so customers can audit who accessed the data and why. Flock’s API and Integrations Terms archived Jan. 6, 2026 say access must be authorized and directed by a Flock customer. Flock’s REST API overview archived July 25, 2026 lists plate lookup, custom hotlists, device locations, FlockOS CAD / alerts / geolocation, and inbound vehicle images. Flock has also said it offers core APIs at no extra cost to law-enforcement agencies archived Aug. 22, 2026 and publishes a partners and integrations archived Aug. 22, 2026 catalog. An API is a way for one computer system to talk to another without an officer clicking through a webpage.
  • Drones on the same stack. Flock’s Drone as First Responder (DFR) archived July 26, 2026 page says docked drones can launch to the coordinates of 911 calls, LPR hits, or gunshot detection, stream live video to officers and dispatch, and “seamlessly integrates with the Flock ecosystem.” Flock Alpha is marketed on that same page. In August 2026 Langley told Business Insider archived Aug. 24, 2026 drones are now Flock’s fastest-growing line, with more than 200 DFR customers, while ALPRs are under half of forward-looking revenue. See CEO / All-In.

That is Flock’s product catalog, not a list of what Crossville has turned on. Crossville has published Falcon units on Quote Q-00234 archived Aug. 18, 2026. It has not published whether APIs, IP-camera ingest, Safe List, drones, or other add-ons are in the live contract. MaryvillePrivacy.org archived Aug. 22, 2026 collected the same class of public Flock product pages for East Tennessee readers.

Questions for City Council: Publish the live agreement and every add-on. Which search tools, sharing settings, APIs, and drone or video products are enabled? Who can authorize an outside system to pull Crossville detections? What happens to the poles and the data if the subscription ends?

Vendor transparency · Aug. 21, 2026 · Gadget Review

Flock blocked the Wayback Machine from saving its own site

Gadget Review archived Aug. 22, 2026 reported August 21, 2026 that Flock’s entire domain is excluded from the Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine. Trying to open flocksafety.com through Wayback returns “This URL has been excluded from the Wayback Machine,” covering the host and its subdomains. Gadget Review says an Internet Archive forum reply attributes that exclusion to a request from the site owners. Blocking a public archive of your own marketing and legal pages is legal. It is not a neutral transparency choice for a company that sells permanent, searchable records of where cars have been.

Gadget Review argues the exclusion makes it harder for cities and residents to independently check when Flock’s public promises change. It lists examples that are now harder to verify on Wayback alone, including shifts in contract language about customer data, post-contract use of footage, liability limits, marketing claims, and retention defaults. Treat those as Gadget Review’s reporting about what is harder to audit, not as this site’s own contract review of Crossville’s agreement.

The same piece contrasts Flock with other surveillance vendors that still have searchable Wayback histories, and recommends treating Wayback archivability of vendor legal terms as a municipal procurement check. Crossville should publish its live Flock agreement and keep dated copies of every vendor page and amendment the city relies on.

Contract fine print · ACLU investigation · Oct. 2025

The "perpetual, worldwide" data license hidden in Flock contracts

Flock's PR line is "we don't sell customer data." The contract says they don't need to — they already have a perpetual license to it.

The ACLU archived Aug. 25, 2026 and ACLU of Massachusetts archived Aug. 30, 2026 found that Flock's template user agreement — signed by police departments across the country — contains this clause:

The customer "hereby grants Flock" a "non-exclusive, worldwide, perpetual, royalty-free right and license" to "disclose the Agency Data… for investigative purposes."

What this means: Even if a department clicks "don't share my data" in Flock's system settings, this contract language may override that choice. The ACLU found this clause in contracts across 80+ Massachusetts police departments. Boston PD is the only major department known to have negotiated the clause out of their agreement.

Raw data vs. "Aggregated Data"

  • Raw plate data and footage: Contractually limited to law enforcement "for investigative purposes only."
  • "Aggregated Data": Flock keeps a perpetual, irrevocable license to use it for "crime prevention efforts, other Flock offerings, and marketing."

The catch: Flock defines what "aggregated" means. Their contract says it's data where personal information has been "permanently anonymized by commercially available standards" — but Flock decides when that standard is met.

The network is already public-private

Flock contracts explicitly list "schools, neighborhood homeowners associations, businesses, and individual users" as "Non-Agency End Users" who can share footage into the same cloud that police search. Private companies are already feeding data into the system — the question isn't whether Flock will sell your data, it's that the database police query already includes private cameras from retailers, HOAs, and security firms across the network.

Questions for City Council: Does Crossville's contract contain the "worldwide, perpetual" license clause? Has the city negotiated it out like Boston did? What data from Crossville cameras can Flock use for "marketing" or "other Flock offerings"? Publish the live agreement.

FreeForm: type a description and search across camera footage

Flock FreeForm™ archived Aug. 21, 2026 is the company’s AI natural-language search product. Instead of only typing a plate, an officer can type a description and pull visual matches from LPR and video, across the agency’s cameras and, where sharing is enabled, partner networks. Crossville residents should treat “just license plate cameras” as incomplete marketing until the city publishes whether FreeForm / people-search / video-search features are enabled on the local contract, ask using our questions for City Council.

  • Vehicles without a plate: plain-language prompts such as “blue SUV with a racing stripe” or “white F-150 with a ladder in the back.”
  • People by appearance: Flock’s own marketing example is “man in blue shirt and cowboy hat.” A company spokesperson has confirmed FreeForm can search by clothing descriptions.
  • Cross-agency reach + alerts: search shared cameras where authorized; get notified when a match reappears, without watching every feed.
  • Flock’s framing: they say FreeForm is not facial recognition, clothing / accessories / vehicle attributes only. Critics note appearance-based search still enables warrantless tracking of people and cars by look across a networked map. Flock’s own U.S. patent filing also describes broader people-classification features; see patent vs marketing.

Multi-Geo & Convoy Search: travel history and who you drive with

Plate reads alone are only the first layer. Flock also sells search tools that turn those hits into cross-city travel queries and maps of which vehicles travel together. East Tennessee public-records releases, summarized by Maryville Residents for Privacy archived Aug. 14, 2026 show Multi-Geo and Convoy Search in regional training materials, including Knox County / East Tennessee task-force context. Crossville has not published whether those features are enabled locally, who can run them, or what written limits apply.

Multi-Geo Search

  • Lets an officer pick two or more cities and find vehicles that appear in all of them inside a chosen time window.
  • Turns scattered camera hits into a single cross-jurisdiction travel query on the shared Flock network.
  • Training examples reference vehicles scanned in both Detroit and Knoxville and flagged for follow-up.
  • Once a plate is in the cloud, appearances anywhere on the networked map can be queried, including for drivers never accused of a crime in Crossville.

Convoy Search

  • Looks for vehicles that repeatedly show up near each other at ALPR cameras.
  • Flags cars as “associated,” so investigators can map who a vehicle tends to travel with.
  • Case examples describe identifying a second “convoy” vehicle and tracking plate changes over time.
  • Runs on correlation alone, no observed criminal act, no warrant, and no notice to the people being linked.
Tennessee training example: probable cause from travel history A case summary in the East Tennessee training materials describes officers reviewing Flock ALPR history showing a vehicle repeatedly scanned in both Detroit and Knoxville, then stating they “got PC”, probable cause, based on that travel pattern and moving to make a stop. The documented “crime” in that writeup was the route, not an observed criminal act. That is tracking someone’s daily routes treated as reason enough to stop them, with serious Fourth Amendment implications for every driver whose plate feeds the same network.

Why Crossville drivers should care:

  • Crossville’s cameras feed the same shared vendor network these tools search when sharing is enabled. Drive to Knoxville, Nashville, or across state lines and the round-trip pattern can become queryable. Documented example: Wisconsin deputies used Flock hits of frequent travel into legal-cannabis Michigan as probable-cause fodder for a weed search (Brown County case).
  • Convoy Search is not only “where you went”, it can sweep in carpools, families, coworkers, and church traffic into a map of who travels with whom, with no link to any crime.
  • As of July 2026, this site has not found a Crossville-published written policy covering Multi-Geo, Convoy, retention for those searches, warrant standards, or public transparency reporting on how often they run.
  • Residents are not notified when their travel pattern or associations are searched or used as an investigative signal.

The ACLU (July 2025) archived Aug. 13, 2026 separately describes Flock Investigations Manager / Multi-State Insights / convoy and multi-location tools marketed to “detect patterns of suspicious activity”, moving from looking up a known plate toward generating suspicion from movement itself. A Norfolk Circuit Court ruling in 2024 treated dense Flock ALPR location collection as a Fourth Amendment search requiring a warrant before use as evidence, the kind of travel-history analysis Multi-Geo and Convoy are built to perform. See also Fourth Amendment and Schmidt v. Norfolk archived July 6, 2026.

Traffic tickets: the “not for moving violations” line is gone

Crossville was sold Flock as extra eyes for investigations, not as a ticket camera. In June 2022 Chief Jessie Brooks told local media the cameras were not red-light ticket devices. Flock’s own materials used to say the cameras “are not used to enforce traffic violations such as speeding, running red lights, or other moving violations.” Streetsblog USA archived Aug. 7, 2026 reported that language disappeared after December 2, 2025. Gadget Review archived Aug. 24, 2026 summarized that shift on August 21, 2026.

The first documented ticket came weeks later. 404 Media archived Aug. 25, 2026 obtained a December 26, 2025 Georgia State Patrol citation whose notes said “CAPTURED ON FLOCK CAMERA 31 MM 1 HOLDING PHONE IN LEFT HAND.” No traffic stop. The agency called it a rare case tied to an expired-registration stop; the citation 404 Media obtained does not mention expired registration. The rider said the ticket was dropped in court. The Electronic Frontier Foundation archived Aug. 25, 2026 treated it as the mission creep privacy groups have warned about: tools sold for serious crime, then used for petty enforcement.

Gadget Review also reports that speed-camera vendors now feed footage into Flock’s cloud under Flock Wing License deals, that Flock sells a Speed Feedback Trailer pairing plates with speed data, and that sequential Flock frames can be used to estimate speed after the fact. Crossville has not published whether any traffic-enforcement, Wing License, or speed-trailer product is enabled here. The 2022 “not ticket cameras” pitch is a promise from a pitch meeting, not a published ban.

Questions for City Council: Is Flock used, or allowed to be used, for moving-violation tickets in Crossville? Is a Flock Wing License, speed-camera ingest, or Speed Feedback Trailer in the live contract? Will council put a written ban on traffic-ticket use in the agreement?

What Flock told the Patent Office vs. what it tells cities

Independent researchers at DeFlock SC archived July 22, 2026 flagged a tension worth checking against primary documents: Flock’s public FAQ says LPR products do not use facial recognition and do not identify race, gender, or ethnicity, while a Flock Group Inc. patent describes people-classification and face-matching features that go much further. A patent does not prove Crossville’s cameras are doing all of that today. It does show what the company told the Patent Office it can build, and what cities should demand in writing.

U.S. Patent US11416545B1 archived Nov. 26, 2025 (“System and method for object based query of video content captured by a dynamic surveillance network”) lists inventors Garrett Langley and Matt Feury, assignee Flock Group Inc., filing date Oct. 4, 2020, and grant / publication date Aug. 16, 2022.

  • What the patent filing describes: AI systems that sort people into groups including “male, female, race, etc.,” plus clothing types and estimated height/weight. In one scenario in the patent, investigators get “face recognition data points” from one camera and search other cameras for matching face recognition data points. That means trying to match the same person across cameras when the computer says the match is close enough, even without a name on file.
  • What the granted legal claims actually say: the numbered claim language talks about object types and object details more generally. It does not spell out “race” or “facial recognition” in the claim text. Race / face wording below is from the long description, not word-for-word claim text.
  • What Flock tells cities (contradiction): Flock’s “Are Flock Products Discriminatory?” archived Aug. 21, 2026 page says LPR cameras “do not use facial recognition,” “do not collect demographic information,” and “do not identify race, gender, or ethnicity.” Its rights & safeguards / civil-liberties page archived Aug. 21, 2026 repeats that ALPR “does not use facial recognition” and “does not collect or infer race, religion, gender, ethnicity, or other personal traits.” FreeForm is marketed as search “without facial recognition” while still offering people-by-clothing examples (FreeForm section).
  • What is already for sale (narrower than the patent’s people demographics): FreeForm’s public product page advertises plain-language people descriptions (clothing) and vehicle attributes across video and plate readers, so appearance search is not hypothetical. Whether race / gender classifiers from the patent filing are turned on is a separate question cities should put in writing.
  • Contract upgrade rights in other cities: Public Flock services agreements commonly let Flock, “in its sole discretion,” make “any upgrades to [the] system or platform that it deems necessary or useful,” stating such upgrades “will not materially change any terms or conditions” of the agreement (example: Glendale, WI Flock agreement §2.12 archived Aug. 21, 2026). Crossville has not published whether its live contract contains the same clause. Ask using our questions for City Council or a TPRA request.

Questions for City Council: Has anyone on council read US11416545B1 archived Nov. 26, 2025? Which people-search / demographic / face-related features are contractually enabled vs. disabled? Can Flock push software upgrades without a new council vote? Will the city publish a written ban on race/gender/ethnicity classification and facial recognition if those features exist in the live product?

Flock’s PR playbook: paid wires, police press-release templates, and sponsored ads

When Crossville first heard about Flock in 2022, the pitch leaned hard on Amber Alerts and finding missing people. That safety framing is part of a bigger nationwide pattern: Flock pushes company talking points into public view through paid publicity, police press kits that agencies have published in substantially similar form, labeled ads in police trade media, and, as of 2026 reporting, a coaching guide for how police should speak to city councils. Here are three long-running channels documented below. (We did not find clear evidence that Flock pays TikTok or Instagram influencers for these stories.) For the newer council-coaching playbook and a related agency SOP that hides ALPR use from drivers, see Own the Narrative and Wapello County.

  1. Flock pays to blast “missing / abducted child” press releases onto the internet. The company sends its own announcements through GlobeNewswire archived June 3, 2022 a service companies pay so their press releases get picked up by news aggregators. Those write-ups then show up on sites like Yahoo Finance archived Aug. 16, 2026 looking a lot like regular news. Examples: Flock’s 2021 AMBER Alert / missing-children partnership announcement archived June 3, 2022 and a 2026 release about six abducted children recovered in Colorado archived Apr. 21, 2026 with Flock plate-reader cameras.

Security failure · Dec. 2025

Open livestreams: no login, found via commercial search engines

In late December 2025, researchers and journalists documented a real security failure that undercuts “secure company cloud” talking points: Flock Condor people-tracking PTZ cameras were left streaming on the open internet with no username, no password, and no encryption. Anyone who found the URL could watch live video, download roughly 30 days of archive, and reach administrative/debug panels.

  • How they were found: Technologist and independent researcher Benn Jordan archived Aug. 3, 2026 located admin interfaces via a commercial device search engine (documented as Shodan archived Aug. 13, 2026 queries such as “Flock Admin”). Researcher Jon “GainSec” Gaines expanded the set with Shodan and ZoomEye archived Aug. 5, 2026 to about 67 live feeds and debug interfaces, see his full write-up archived Aug. 4, 2026. Jordan’s report describes finding the interfaces “using a commercial search engine”, not hacking into a locked system.
  • Scale: 404 Media archived Aug. 12, 2026 verified at least ~60 Condor cameras exposed nationwide; GainSec catalogued 67. Feeds covered playgrounds, parking lots, bike paths, street corners, retail lots, and more. The Verge archived Aug. 15, 2026 summarized the same findings.
  • Independent proof: 404 Media’s Jason Koebler recorded himself crossing a street median at Harris Road & Young Street in Bakersfield, California, and watched himself on the open livestream while colleagues watched remotely. Jordan filmed himself on Peachtree Creek Greenway feeds that auto-tracked people.
  • What was open: live stream + ~30-day archive download + settings/logs/diagnostics: “like Netflix,” in Jordan’s framing, with the ability to delete footage from the open admin UI (he reported not doing so).

“On my phone, I am watching myself in real time as the camera records and livestreams me, without any password or login, to the open internet.”

Jason Koebler, 404 Media archived Aug. 12, 2026 (Bakersfield Condor verification)

This site does not re-host the exposed camera stills (including playground feeds). Watch the primary reporting: 404 Media archived Aug. 12, 2026 (Koebler’s Bakersfield self-record) and Benn Jordan’s video archived Aug. 3, 2026. The Verge archived Aug. 15, 2026 summarized the same findings.

Flock called it a “limited misconfiguration” on a “very small number of devices” and said it was remedied; some agency emails blamed cellular/SIM testing configurations (WFLX / company confirmation archived June 1, 2026). 51 cybersecurity findings (GainSec whitepaper)

In November 2025, independent researcher Jon “GainSec” Gaines published a formal whitepaper: Examining the Security Posture of an Anti-Crime Ecosystem archived Aug. 21, 2026 consolidating device-level research on Flock’s public-safety stack (license-plate readers, audio/gunshot hardware, compute modules, and related Android apps). The current public release documents 51 findings, including 22 assigned CVEs and additional findings pending CVE assignment, covering authentication, cryptography, and system-design issues (GitHub archive archived June 3, 2026 · GainSec summary archived Aug. 4, 2026).

This is separate from the Dec. 2025 open Condor livestream exposure. The whitepaper is about weaknesses in the product line itself, the kind of cameras cities lease, not a claim that Crossville’s units were exploited.

  • Hardcoded credentials: CVE-2025-47823 archived July 24, 2026 describes a hardcoded system password on Flock LPR firmware through version 2.2 (NVD / MITRE).
  • Unauthenticated admin API: CVE-2025-59403 archived Apr. 4, 2026 is a publicly catalogued Flock camera admin-API issue also cited in this site’s liability table.
  • Device-level control (physical access context): GainSec’s series documents root/debug access paths on Falcon/Sparrow LPR units and related hardware when an attacker can reach the device. Flock’s public response stresses that exploitation would require physical access plus intimate knowledge of internal hardware, and says findings were disclosed earlier in 2025 with CVE registration and ongoing remediations ( Flock response archived Aug. 21, 2026).
  • Account / MFA risk (oversight, not a GainSec device CVE): A Nov. 2025 congressional letter asked the FTC to investigate Flock’s cybersecurity practices, including that MFA was not required and that weak MFA methods remained supported ( Wyden-Krishnamoorthi FTC letter, PDF archived Aug. 10, 2026).

Flock states the compiled findings do not impair customers’ public-safety use, that no customer action is required because Flock owns/maintains the devices, and that remediations continue via hardware and software updates. Residents can still ask whether Crossville received written notice, what firmware/security posture applies to local units, and what breach-notification terms sit in the live contract.

Problems with Axon too

Some cities hire Axon after dumping Flock because Axon says it is safer or more private. That sales pitch does not stop abuse. Axon products raise separate transparency, conflict-of-interest, and false-alert concerns documented below. None of this is a reason to bring Axon, or any plate-reader company, to Crossville.

Axon · Draft One · AI police reports · 2024 to 2025

Draft One: AI writes the police report, then deletes the draft

Axon’s Draft One uses body-cam audio to write a draft police report. EFF archived July 3, 2026 and Ars Technica archived Nov. 25, 2025 reported that the original AI draft is not kept, so it is hard to compare versions or prove what the AI wrote. Agencies can also turn off a check meant to make sure officers actually read the draft, also covered by Mother Jones archived July 28, 2026 and the ACLU’s Automated Police Reports brief (PDF) archived June 29, 2026. Generative-AI tools can produce inaccurate content. If the draft is gone, courts and defense lawyers have a harder time seeing what the machine said versus what the officer signed.

Axon Fusus · Atlanta ethics · conflict of interest

Atlanta ethics finding: police official owned a Fusus stake while working city business without required disclosure

The Atlanta Ethics Office archived Jan. 20, 2026 found APD Chief Administrative Officer Marshall Freeman consulted for Fusus, owned a stake in the company, and worked on Fusus city business without proper disclosure, including pitching it to other agencies (Atlanta Press Collective archived Aug. 8, 2026; The Guardian archived Aug. 9, 2026). Axon Fleet 3 plate reader · false alerts

Axon’s help pages describe false alerts and outdated stolen-car lists

The Fleet 3 ALPR FAQ archived Apr. 29, 2026 tells officers how to mark alerts as Incorrect or Wrong State/Province. Axon’s snooze guidance archived Apr. 18, 2026 describes procedures for handling incorrect and wrong-state alerts: older software often cannot tell which state a plate is from (so the same characters from another state can trigger a false alert), can sometimes “read” things that are not plates, and depends on stolen-car lists that can be out of date. Multiple plate-reader companies · LAPD Inspector General · 2026

LAPD: about 1 in 3 stolen-car alerts were wrong. Axon cameras were part of the system

LAPD’s Inspector General looked at two months of alerts. Axon can be abused the same way Flock is

Documented Flock and ALPR problems, personal lookups with no case, misread plates, outdated stolen-car lists, are not unique to one company. They happen when police get a searchable log of where cars go, alerts that feel like “stop that car now,” and little day-to-day supervision. Axon’s in-car readers, pole cameras, Evidence storage, and Fusus real-time tools can do the same things, and can pull in body cams, private-camera feeds, and AI report writing too. That is why Crossville should not switch companies, and why Crossville should take these cameras down completely and stay away from the same kind of technology under any other name.

Same problems with Flock or Axon

  • Personal stalking: An officer looks up an ex’s plate or travel history, the Institute for Justice archived Aug. 15, 2026 has documented dozens of these cases; which company sold the cameras does not matter much
  • Searches with no real case: Looking up yourself, family, or coworkers for no official reason: AJC / GBI reporting (2026) archived July 19, 2026 describes Georgia agencies finding misuse when they actually checked the logs
  • False or outdated stolen-car alerts: A misread plate or a car no longer stolen still triggers a stop if nobody checks
  • Wrong-state or similar-looking plates: Axon’s own docs describe Wrong State hits; Flock misreads are widely reported, both put innocent drivers at risk
  • Sharing with outsiders: Local data can still leave the agency through partners and APIs unless the cameras are gone
  • Logs that do not stop abuse: Search logs exist; misuse is usually found by victims or rare audits, not by company marketing

Axon adds more problems, not more privacy

  • More cameras, more places: Readers in patrol cars plus pole/streetlight cameras mean more of your trips get logged
  • Fusus real-time screen: Combines live video and plate hits; warrant rules still depend on agency policy and law
  • Evidence.com: Plate records sit with body-cam video in the same Axon system, harder for a city to leave Axon entirely
  • Draft One: AI writes report drafts, then deletes them, harder to see what the machine produced
  • Ring + fususCORE: Doorbell requests and resident-purchased private-camera hardware extend the network into homes (Ring, Connect Knoxville, Community Connect directory)
  • Bigger packages: Places like Douglas County swap Flock for Axon and add drones, more surveillance while saying “we canceled Flock”

Leonardo ELSAG · SignalTrace · June 2026 reporting

Leonardo SignalTrace, plate readers that also scoop phones and Bluetooth IDs

Leonardo (ELSAG ALPR line) markets SignalTrace as a retrofit for license-plate cameras: sensors capture identifiers from nearby phones, wearables, Wi-Fi sources, RFID tags (even pet microchips in product materials), and vehicle systems, then correlate recurring “electronic fingerprints” with plate reads and locations. Company materials say the system can help identify a vehicle even without a readable plate. Reporting by 404 Media archived Aug. 13, 2026 and Techdirt archived Aug. 8, 2026 describes this as turning car trackers into people trackers, mass collection first, investigative query later.

Why Crossville should care: a “not Flock” camera deal can still expand into device-sensing ALPR. Ask in writing whether any vendor has pitched SignalTrace, ELSAG, or Bluetooth/Wi-Fi vehicle correlation, and refuse the whole stack.

Motorola Solutions · Vigilant / LEARN · long-running ALPR network

Motorola Vigilant, enterprise plate databases and sharing fights

Motorola Solutions acquired Vigilant Solutions and operates large ALPR networks and the LEARN-style sharing platforms used by many agencies. EFF’s ALPR research found agencies sharing Vigilant data with large numbers of other entities on average. In 2024, CISA published advisories on Vigilant ALPR security flaws (weak credentials / encryption issues). In July 2026, a California class action (amended complaint) alleged Motorola/Vigilant facilitated unlawful out-of-state and federal sharing of California ALPR data, allegations in a filed complaint, not a court judgment.

LAPD’s multi-vendor ALPR stack (Flock, Motorola, and Axon named in Inspector General coverage) shows cities already mix these brands. Quitting Flock for Motorola is still mass scanning.

What to tell City Council

  • Cancel Flock and remove every camera. Do not evaluate, demo, trial, or install Axon, Leonardo ELSAG / SignalTrace, Motorola Vigilant, Rekor, Genetec, Verkada, or any other plate-reader system, ever again.
